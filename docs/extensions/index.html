Both extensions carry the theory over formally, not as theorems: a change of clock for unknown reliability, and one kernel per bound when the rewards differ. The paper's evidence for each is numerical. Every plate in this chapter keeps that frame: the equations are the paper's, carried over without proof, and the numbers that judge them are measurements.

When the strength of the evidence is unknown, the belief moves by the same rule at every moment once time is counted in units of information, but on that clock a constant cost of time rises without bound, outside the costs the theorems allow. When the two correct answers pay differently, the problem no longer folds in half: there are two bounds, and each looks ahead with its own kernel. Nothing here extends the proof of Theorem 7.

When the evidence's strength is unknown

So far the evidence has drifted at +1 or −1, and only the sign was unknown. Now let the strength be unknown too. The drift is μ∼N(0,1/a), drawn once per trial and never shown, the noise has variance 1 per unit time, and a choice is correct when it matches the sign of μ. After time t, the evidence Xt=μt+Wt makes the decision maker's belief about μ normal, with mean Xt/(t+a) and variance 1/(t+a), so the chance that μ>0 is

P(μ>0∣Xt)=Φ(u),u=Xtt+a.

In the decision maker's own view, the evidence drifts at its current estimate of μ: dX=X/(t+a)dt+dW¯, where W¯ is a Brownian motion. That rule depends on t: the same evidence counts for less late in a trial, because a drift that has had a long time to show itself, and hasn't, is probably small.

A change of clock removes the dependence. In information time θ=log⁡((t+a)/a), a change of clock that Campbell and Engelund (2026, Example 8.7) already use for a Gaussian unknown drift, as the paper notes, the belief variable moves by the same rule at every moment:

du=12udθ+dWθ.

The cost changes with the clock. A stretch dt of time is dθ=dt/(t+a) of information time, so a cost c(t) per unit time is κ(θ)=c(t)(t+a) per unit of information time. Before the plate draws it on your schedule, a guess: what does a constant cost become?

A constant cost c becomes κ=caeθ, which rises without bound: each unit of information takes longer to arrive than the one before. That puts the problem outside the costs between two positive constants that Proposition 3, Proposition 4, and Theorem 7 assume (the standing assumption, chapter 3), so the paper carries their structure over formally, not as a theorem.

The bound and the kernel, carried over

On the information clock the belief is a time-homogeneous diffusion, so the methods of chapters 3 to 6 can be carried over to it, formally. For a constant κ, the problem is a stationary one, and the paper finds its bound the way chapter 3 found B(c). On u>0 the reward for deciding, Φ(u), is harmonic for the belief's generator (Φ(|u|) has a kink at 0; there the value has slope 0 by symmetry, which is the condition the calculation uses), and the value's equation with smooth fit at U gives

∫0Uev2/2dv=122πκ.

This is an instance of Remark 5, the identity chapter 5's plate “Same algebra, two diffusions” draws for both: for the belief, the scale density is s′=e−u2/2, the speed density is m=2eu2/2, and Φ′/s′=1/2π. For a κ that changes, the exact equation would become

∫0U(θ)2eu2/2Eu[κ(θ+τ∗)]du=12π,

and the argument of Theorem 7, applied formally, gives a kernel: the decision time of u started from its speed measure normalized on [0,U], which is its survival flux at U, normalized. The paper computes it from a finite-difference generator and runs the weighted look-ahead on the information clock. Scrub through the derivation; the plate beside it draws each step.

What is derived, and what is carried over. The posterior is exact, and so are the change of clock and the cost per unit of information time. The constant-κ bound is the paper's calculation from the value's equation and smooth fit, for a cost that is constant on the information clock (in real time, c(t)=κ0/(t+a)). The exact equation for a changing κ, the kernel, and the weighted rule built on it are carried over formally: the paper proves no analog of Proposition 3 or Theorem 7 for this process, and a constant cost in real time is outside the bounded costs those results assume. The evidence for the rule is the measurement in Table 10. One reading note on the excerpt: its “Φ(|u|) is harmonic” holds on u>0 (and on u<0); at 0, Φ(|u|) has a kink, and the calculation uses the value's slope there, 0 by symmetry, not the reward's.

The rules in information time

Three rules need no dynamic program. The frozen-time rule sets the bound at each moment to U(κ(θ)), the constant-κ bound for the current cost per unit of information: it is the per-moment rule of chapter 1, on the information clock. The weighted look-ahead runs Algorithm 1 on the same clock: it averages the frozen-time bounds ahead with the u-kernel for its own level, by 30 damped updates. The plug-in rule treats the current estimate of the drift's size, |X|/(t+a), as if it were known, and stops where the known-reliability bound for that drift says to. The paper adds a fourth, a constant evidence bound tuned with the scorer: its level is chosen to maximize the scored reward, so it is tuned on the score it is judged by. The first three give an evidence bound X(t), moved in by 0.5826Δt for checking every Δt=0.005.

The reference is the exact policy, from a dynamic program over a grid of 300 beliefs that steps back from a forced choice at time 10 (or at the deadline). On the paper's eight schedules with a=1, the weighted rule loses at most 0.056 points per 100 trials, and the frozen-time rule up to 0.93. At constant costs of 0.02 and 0.05, the tuned constant bound does as well as the weighted rule or slightly better: it loses 0.049 and 0.020, against 0.056 and 0.021. The reference comes from a grid of beliefs, not from the scorer's own grid, so a rule can score a little above it: losses are no longer guaranteed to be nonnegative, and the paper has no error bound for small differences between rules.

Two things to look for in the plate. Even at a constant cost, the exact bound moves over the trial, because the cost per unit of information rises; the frozen-time rule, which looks only at the present κ, starts above it on all eight schedules. And the plug-in rule fails in its own way: when the estimate of the drift is small, the known-reliability rule for that small drift says waiting isn't worth its cost: the plug-in bound is 0, so the rule stops at once, wherever 4c(t)(t+a)≥1; the plate's bound, moved in for checks every 0.005, reaches 0 a little before that.

Unequal rewards: two bounds

Back to a drift of ±1, but now a correct “+” earns r+>0 and a correct “−” earns r−>0, and errors earn nothing. At evidence x, choosing “+” earns r+g(x) on average and choosing “−” earns r−(1−g(x)), so the two are worth the same at the indifference point

x∗=12log⁡(r−/r+),

which is below 0 when “+” pays more. The problem no longer folds in half at 0: the paper describes the waiting region as an interval (ℓ(t),h(t)) around x∗, with “+” chosen at h and “−” at ℓ. For a constant cost, Appendix B gives a pair of explicit equations for the two bounds.

A guess before the plate: if a correct “−” pays a quarter of a correct “+”, which bound moves more from where equal rewards put it, at ±B(c)?

In the plate's computations, at every cost it offers (0.01 to 1) and every ratio below 1 (down to 0.05), the upper bound moves more; at a ratio of 1, neither moves. It comes down, so the decision maker chooses “+” with less certainty than before: a wrong “+” forfeits only the small reward for “−”. The lower bound, where it chooses “−” and a mistake forfeits the large reward for “+”, moves down too, by less. At high costs or lopsided enough rewards, the upper bound falls below 0, and then the decision maker chooses “+” at the start, without waiting.

Appendix B derives the pair from the value's equation on the waiting region, smooth fit at each end, and value matching across it. Scrub through it.

One kernel per bound

When the cost changes a little, c0+εγ(t), how do the two bounds move? The paper applies the envelope argument of chapter 5 at each bound separately. To first order, the change costs the decision maker εγ for as long as it stays undecided, and at each bound the meeting point of waiting and deciding shifts by that change's slope divided by the curvature gap 2c0. The result:

δh(t)≈−ε2c0∫0∞γ(t+s)nh(s)ds,nh(s)=−∂xPx(τ>s)|x=h0,

and symmetrically at ℓ, where τ is the time to leave (ℓ0,h0). Each bound looks ahead with its own survival flux, and the two decouple at first order. With both rewards equal to 1, both fluxes are chapter 6's, and the formula is Theorem 7's first-order term.

Where the theory stops. Appendix B's equations follow from the value's equation, smooth fit at both ends, and value matching, for a waiting region that is one interval around x∗, as the paper describes it; Proposition 3 proves smooth fit only for equal rewards, so Appendix B assumes it at both bounds. The first-order part, one kernel per bound, is a formal per-bound envelope argument that the paper says it has not made rigorous. Theorem 7 itself assumes equal rewards, a drift of known size, and a cost between two positive constants; nothing in this chapter extends its proof, and both extensions are listed among the paper's limitations.

Where this chapter sits

The paper's claims and the places one uses another, with this chapter's two highlighted. Unknown reliability uses Remark 5 (chapter 5) for the constant-κ bound and the kernel's starting law, the argument of Theorem 7 (chapter 6), which it applies formally, and the weighted look-ahead rule (chapter 8), which it runs on the information clock. Unequal rewards uses the envelope identity (Lemma 2) and smooth fit, which Proposition 3 proves for equal rewards and Appendix B assumes at both bounds, from chapter 5, and Theorem 7, which it reproduces when both rewards are 1. Exercises

Worked answers are folded under each question. Nothing is gated.

  1. Check that κ(θ)=c(t)(t+a) and that a constant cost grows exponentially in θ.

    Worked answer

    θ=log⁡((t+a)/a) gives dθ=dt/(t+a), so a stretch dt of real time is dθ=dt/(t+a) of information time, and its cost c(t)dt is c(t)(t+a)dθ. Since t+a=aeθ, a constant c gives κ(θ)=caeθ: exponential growth. Information about μ arrives ever more slowly, so each unit of it takes more real time, and costs more.

  2. Derive the posterior Φ(X/t+a).

    Worked answer

    With prior μ∼N(0,1/a) and evidence Xt=μt+Wt, the posterior of μ is normal with precision a+t and mean Xt/(t+a). So P(μ>0∣Xt)=Φ((Xt/(t+a))t+a)=Φ(Xt/t+a). The drift in the decision maker's view is the posterior mean, X/(t+a).

  3. Show that the cost c(t)=κ0/(t+a) is constant on the information clock. What do the frozen-time and weighted rules do for it?

    Worked answer

    κ(θ)=c(t)(t+a)=κ0 at every moment. The weighted rule averages U(κ(θ+s)) over s with weights that sum to 1, and every term is U(κ0), so the average is U(κ0) whatever the kernel: the two rules give the same bound, U(κ0)t+a in evidence units. (A test in the paper's code checks this.) It is the information-clock version of a constant cost for known reliability.

  4. Why does the weighted rule sit below the frozen-time rule for a constant cost?

    Worked answer

    A constant c gives κ(θ+s)=caeθ+s>κ(θ) for every s>0, and U falls as κ rises, so every term U(κ(θ+s)) the weighted rule averages is below the frozen-time bound U(κ(θ)), and so is their average. The rule looks ahead at a cost that will only rise and lowers its bound now.

  5. Why should small differences between rules in Table 10 be read with caution?

    Worked answer

    The reference policy is a dynamic program over 300 belief bins, not the scorer's own grid, so a rule can score slightly better than the reference, and the paper has no error bound for the gap. The large differences (frozen-time against weighted, or the plug-in rule's losses) are clear; differences of a few thousandths are not.

  6. Derive the indifference point x∗=12log⁡(r−/r+).

    Worked answer

    At x, choosing “+” earns r+g(x) in expectation and choosing “−” earns r−(1−g(x)). They are equal when g/(1−g)=r−/r+. Since g/(1−g)=e2x, x∗=12log⁡(r−/r+). With r−<r+, x∗<0: evidence a little below 0 still makes “+” the better choice. Whether the decision maker stops there depends on where the bounds are.

  7. Show that the bounds of Appendix B satisfy S(h)−S(ℓ)=(r++r−)/(2c).

    Worked answer

    Smooth fit gives cS(h)+K=12r+ and cS(ℓ)+K=−12r−. Subtracting removes K: c(S(h)−S(ℓ))=12(r++r−). Since S is increasing, the gap in S fixes ℓ once h is chosen, and value matching then picks h.

  8. Appendix B says a common reward r gives h=−ℓ=B(c/r). Why?

    Worked answer

    Multiplying both rewards by r and the cost by r multiplies the whole objective by r, which doesn't change the best rule. So rewards r with cost c behave like rewards 1 with cost c/r, whose bound is B(c/r).

  9. Check that the per-bound formula reproduces Theorem 7 in the symmetric case.

    Worked answer

    With r+=r−=1, nh=n, and ∫n=1/(2c0cosh2⁡b0) (Lemma 6(a)). Write n=(∫n)w: the shift is −ε2c0∫n⋅(w⋆γ)=−ε4c02cosh2⁡b0(w⋆γ)=εB′(c0)(w⋆γ), which is the first-order term εβ of Theorem 7.

Common misreadings.

  • “The paper proves the theory for unknown reliability.”It carries the exact equation and the kernel over formally and checks the rule numerically. It has not proved analogs of Proposition 3 or Theorem 7 for this process, and says so.
  • “A constant cost stays constant on the information clock.”It becomes κ=caeθ, which rises without bound. The frozen-time rule, which looks only at the present κ, ignores that rise; at a constant cost of 0.02 it loses 0.825 points per 100 trials.
  • “The weighted rule beats every alternative here.”At constant costs of 0.02 and 0.05, the tuned constant bound does as well or slightly better.
  • “The unequal-rewards result is a theorem.”It is a formal per-bound envelope argument with numerical checks; the paper says it has not made it rigorous.
  • “With unequal rewards, both bounds shift by the same amount toward the better-paid answer.”The upper bound, where the better-paid answer is chosen, moves more than the lower one, and both move down; the plate on two bounds computes how much for ratios from 0.05 to 1 and costs from 0.01 to 1.
  • “There is a table of the unequal-rewards results.”The paper prints two of them, the largest losses, in its text; the per-family numbers are in tab_lopsided.tex, which its code writes but the paper doesn't print.
