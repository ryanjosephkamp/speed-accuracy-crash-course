On schedules fixed in advance and run once, the per-moment rule gives up little reward, and the weighted look-ahead avoids most of that loss. This chapter reads the paper's measurements of the two rules where its theorems don't reach: large changes, steps, pulses, and deadlines. First come the 90 development schedules the rule was designed on, then 210 held-out schedules, specified in advance and run once, with five more rules for comparison and a table of how far each rule's bound is from the optimal one. Then how much reward the per-moment rule gives up, and the paper's two tests of its conjectured loss formula.

Every number here is a measurement. Each loss is scored by pushing the evidence density forward on a grid, which gives the distribution of decision times, with no sampling; it is in points per 100 trials, against the dynamic program's best policy on the same grid (chapter 9). The plates draw the paper's own results, schedule by schedule, from its results files at the tag. The page also scores your schedule, and any development schedule you tap, with the same rules on a coarser grid, and labels those numbers as its own.

Ninety schedules the rule was designed on

The theorems of chapters 6 to 8 are about small changes in a continuous cost. The paper's random schedules test the two rules beyond them. It drew 90 development schedules from seed 2026, the seed used while the weighted rule was designed, 30 in each of three families:

  • Smooth: log⁡c(t)=log⁡c0+∑j=13ajsin⁡(ωjt+ϕj), with c0 log-uniform on [0.02,0.5], each aj uniform on [−23,23], each ωj uniform on [0.3,6], and each phase uniform.
  • Step: a jump from c0 to c0r at a time t0, logistic in time with scale 0.05, with c0 log-uniform on [0.02,1], r log-uniform on [116,16], and t0 uniform on [0.1,2].
  • Deadline: a constant cost c0, log-uniform on [0.01,0.5], and a forced choice at a time T uniform on [0.3,3].

Without a deadline, the choice is forced at t=10. The per-moment rule loses up to 2.41 points per 100 trials; the weighted look-ahead loses at most 0.083. Measured as total loss, the weighted rule recovers 97.9% of the per-moment rule's loss on smooth schedules, 97.8% on steps, and 95.5% on deadlines. That share is one minus the ratio of the family's two total losses, so the schedules with large losses count the most.

Guess first: in which family does the weighted rule recover the smallest share?

Deadlines are where the weighted rule recovers the least, and where its median loss is largest: 0.0091 points per 100 trials, against 0.0012 on smooth schedules and 0.0008 on steps. Its largest single loss, 0.083, is on a step. In each family, its median loss is less than a tenth of the per-moment rule's.

One caution goes with every number in this section. These schedules come from the seed used while the rule was designed, so a good result on them could partly reflect choices made while looking at them. That is why the paper ran a second set.

210 schedules, fixed in advance and run once

To test the rule on schedules it wasn't shaped by, the paper specified a held-out set before running it, and ran it once: seed 20261111, 210 schedules, 30 in each of seven families. Three are the development families, drawn again with the new seed, and four are new:

  • Pulse: a Gaussian bump in log⁡c that changes the cost by up to 8-fold either way.
  • Ramp: log⁡c changing linearly over a random time, so that the cost changes by up to 16-fold either way, then flat.
  • Multi-step: two to four steps, each changing the cost by up to 4-fold either way.
  • Piecewise: a cost constant on random intervals, about 0.45 time units long on average, at levels drawn independently and log-uniformly from [0.02,1].

Multi-step and piecewise schedules have their joins smoothed in log⁡c by the same logistic as the steps. The per-moment rule loses a median of 0.13 points per 100 trials (0.16% of the reward) and at most 4.67; the weighted look-ahead loses at most 0.235.

Five more rules

The paper also scored five more rules on the held-out schedules: four shortcuts that the theory of chapters 6 to 8 might suggest, and an oracle to compare them with:

  • Weighted, one pass: the weighted look-ahead's average of the per-moment bounds ahead, taken once, with the kernel for the per-moment bound and no iteration.
  • Averaged cost: bavg(t)=B((wc(t)⋆c)(t)), the bound for the kernel-weighted average of the coming cost (chapter 8).
  • Shift by the mean look-ahead: B(c(t+m(c(t)))), the per-moment rule for the cost one mean look-ahead later, where m(c) is the kernel's mean at the cost c.
  • Shift by 0.5: B(c(t+12)). Half a time unit is the limit of the mean look-ahead as the cost goes to 0 (Corollary 9(b)).
  • Tuned constant: a single flat evidence bound for each schedule, chosen by searching with the scorer itself on that schedule. The paper calls it an oracle rather than a usable rule.

Reading the panels with Table 6:

  • Both versions of the weighted look-ahead lose less than the per-moment rule on every one of the 180 (180 / 0 and 180 / 0). A single pass loses up to 1.29, against 0.235 for the iterated rule, so the iteration matters in the worst cases.
  • Averaging costs beats the per-moment rule on 169 of the 180, but fails on deadlines, with losses up to 42.64, as Section 5 predicts. Chapter 8 explains why: in an average of costs, the infinite cost past a deadline drives the bound to 0 (in the paper's code, once the deadline comes within reach of its binned kernels).
  • Shifting by the mean look-ahead is worse than not shifting at all on 83 of the 180, so the kernel's shape matters, not only its mean. A shift reads the cost at a single delay; the weighted rule averages the bounds over every delay with the kernel's weights.
  • Even the tuned constant beats the per-moment rule on only 62 of them: the best flat bound the search finds for a schedule usually does worse than a bound that follows the cost.

Close in reward, wrong in timing

The per-moment rule's bound is wrong at first order. For a small change εγ around a constant cost, its error divided by ε tends to B′(c0)(γ−w⋆γ), which isn't the zero function unless γ is constant, though it can vanish at particular times or on whole intervals: where γ stays constant from some time on, for example, w⋆γ equals it there (chapter 6). In reward, though, it is already close to optimal. That is consistent with Proposition 10, a statement about small changes in the continuous model: from a fixed start y0 with 0≤y0<b0, a policy that stops when the evidence first reaches a continuous bound within a constant times ε of b0 loses reward only of order ε2, because the reward is flat at its peak (chapter 7).

The held-out schedules measure it for changes that aren't small. The per-moment rule gives up a median of 0.16% of the reward. It loses under 0.1 point per 100 trials on 87 of the 210 schedules, and 1 or more on only 11, up to 4.67 on the worst.

What the look-ahead changes is where the bound sits and when it moves. So, in the words that end the first paragraph of the paper's Section 9, the look-ahead's “clearest test is not accuracy or reward, but the timing of an urgency signal” (chapter 12).

Testing a conjecture

This section tests a conjecture. The paper states Conjecture 11 without proof (chapter 7). The two tables here are measurements that test it, not proofs of it.

Conjecture 11 is about a miss of fixed shape. For a bounded, Lipschitz f, a policy whose bound misses the best bound by η=εf should lose E[c(τ^)η(τ^)2] of reward, up to o(ε2), where τ^ is its decision time. The rules' misses change shape with ε, so the conjecture gives no order for their losses. The paper's test applies the formula to each rule's actual miss on a grid, as a sum over the grid times tj:

predicted loss=100∑jfjc(tj)η(tj)2,

in points per 100 trials, with fj the chance of deciding at tj under the rule, computed on the grid without sampling, and η the rule's distance from the optimal bound. The sum leaves out the last grid time, t=10: there the scorer forces every undecided trial to choose, whatever either bound says, so a gap between the bounds costs nothing.

The paper tests it on 36 schedules from seed 7: six smooth schedules at each of three sizes and six steps at each of three ratios, with base costs log-uniform on [0.03,0.3]. A smooth schedule of size a has its three amplitudes of log⁡c uniform on [−23a,23a]; the steps alternate up and down. At the largest ratio, 16-fold, the per-moment rule's bound error is no longer small. Guess first: does the formula over- or underestimate the per-moment rule's loss there?

For the per-moment rule, the formula overestimates the loss by 1 to 5% for smooth schedules and for steps up to threefold, and by 36% for the 16-fold steps. For the weighted rule, whose total loss is about 50 times smaller, it overestimates by a factor of 1.04 to 1.55. At the smallest size of each family, most of the weighted rule's prediction comes from t>8, where the optimal bound collapses toward the forced choice at t=10 and the rules, which ignore it, don't. There, the gap isn't small, so the formula need not hold, and that part of the prediction compares rules that ignore the horizon with the finite-horizon optimum. So Table 8 tests the formula for the per-moment rule and is weak evidence about the weighted rule.

How the loss grows with the change

Each row of Table 8 draws new schedules, so it can't show how a loss grows with the size of the change. Table 9 does, for one smooth shape scaled in log⁡c around c0=0.2, where decisions are short and the forced choice at t=10 hardly matters:

c(t)=0.2eaf(t),f(t)=0.6sin⁡(1.3t+0.4)+0.5sin⁡(3.1t+2)+0.4sin⁡(5.2t+4).

Each amplitude is scored on the paper's grid and again on one twice as fine in time and space. If a loss grows like ap, doubling a multiplies it by 2p: by 4 for a loss of second order, and by 16 for one of fourth order. Guess the two growth factors before the plate draws the points.

From a=0.05 to 0.4, each doubling multiplies the per-moment rule's loss by 3.5 to 4.0, close to the 4 of a second-order loss; its effective order over that range, the slope of log loss against log amplitude, is 1.9. The weighted rule's loss is far smaller. The paper reads its order only from a=0.2 up, where the two grids agree to within 29% at a=0.2 and better above. At a=0.1, they happen to agree more closely, on a loss under 10−6 points per 100 trials, and at the smallest amplitude they differ even in sign. A loss of 10−6 points per 100 trials is a difference of 10−8 in the reward per trial, so a small change in the grid can move such a loss by as much as its size. From a=0.2 to 0.8, its effective order is 3.7 on one grid and 3.9 on the other. That is consistent with the heuristic fourth order of chapter 7, but it is a measurement over a short range, not an asymptotic result.

The page's own grid is coarser than both. Run the shape on it, and at the three smallest amplitudes its weighted losses differ from the paper's grid in sign or by more than half; the plate's sentence gives both grids' signed values. That is a comparison of two grids, not a measure of what either one resolves. At the constant cost, where the per-moment rule would be optimal on an infinite horizon with continuous checking, this grid scores it a small negative loss; that is one signed difference at one cost, not an error bound for the other amplitudes.

What these two tests show, and what they don't. Table 8 supports Conjecture 11 for the per-moment rule at moderate sizes, on 36 schedules, with the formula applied to misses that change shape, outside the conjecture's fixed-shape statement; it doesn't prove it, and it is weak evidence about the weighted rule. Neither is an asymptotic statement.

Where this chapter sits

The paper's claims and the places one uses another, with this chapter's five highlighted: the development schedules, the held-out schedules with the five more rules, the per-moment rule's closeness in reward, and the two tests of the loss formula.

Exercises

Worked answers are folded under each question. Nothing is gated.

  1. What does “recovers 97.9% … measured as total loss” mean, and how could it differ from a per-schedule average?

    Worked answer

    It is one minus the ratio of totals over the family: 1−∑(weighted losses)/∑(per-moment losses). Schedules where the per-moment rule loses a lot dominate the totals. A per-schedule figure (the median of 1−weighted/per-moment, as the held-out set reports it, over the schedules where the per-moment rule loses at least 0.01) weights each of those schedules equally, and can be lower or higher.

  2. Why does it matter that the 90 are development schedules?

    Worked answer

    The paper says they come from the seed used while the rule was designed, so good results on them could partly reflect choices made while looking at these schedules. The held-out set exists to test the rule on schedules it wasn't shaped by (the held-out set).

  3. The held-out paragraph mentions 180 schedules twice. Are they the same schedules?

    Worked answer

    No. The first is the schedules where the per-moment rule loses at least 0.01 point per 100 trials (180). The second is the schedules without a deadline (180: six families of 30). The paper says they are different sets of the same size by coincidence.

  4. Read Table 6. Which rules beat the per-moment rule on every schedule without a deadline, and which on fewer than half?

    Worked answer

    The weighted look-ahead and its single-pass version: their wins and losses are 180 / 0 and 180 / 0, so each loses less than the per-moment rule on all 180. Averaging costs has 169 / 11. Shifting by the mean look-ahead (97 / 83) wins on just over half; shifting by half a time unit (80 / 100) and the tuned constant (62 / 118) beat it on fewer than half.

  5. Why is the tuned constant called an oracle?

    Worked answer

    For each schedule, its single flat bound is tuned separately, by searching with the scorer itself on that schedule. The paper calls it an oracle because it is the best flat bound found by scoring that very schedule, a benchmark rather than a rule built from the theory. It shows that the per-moment rule's changes over time are worth having: even the best flat bound the search found beats it on only 62 of the 180 schedules without a deadline.

  6. Read Table 7's deadline row. How far does each rule's bound stray, and what happens to accuracy and decision time?

    Worked answer

    On deadlines, the per-moment bound's largest gap from the optimal bound has a median of 1.437 evidence units, against 0.118 for the weighted rule. The per-moment rule's accuracy changes by a median of +0.29 percentage points and its mean decision time by +0.133, against −0.11 and −0.012 for the weighted rule. These are medians over the family, relative to the optimal policy.

  7. If the per-moment rule loses so little, why does the paper matter?

    Worked answer

    Three reasons. The look-ahead changes where the bound sits and when it moves, which the paper names as its clearest test: the timing of an urgency signal (chapter 12). It explains what the dynamic program computes: how far ahead the best bound looks, and with what weights (chapters 6 and 7). And the worst cases are not small: up to 4.67 points per 100 trials on the held-out set.

  8. Why does the formula's prediction leave out t=10?

    Worked answer

    At the last grid time, the scorer forces every undecided trial to choose by the sign of its evidence, whatever either bound says, so two different bounds there give the same choice and the same reward: a gap between them costs nothing. Including that time would charge the formula for gaps that have no effect.

  9. Why does Table 8 say little about the weighted rule?

    Worked answer

    Its losses are tiny, and at the smallest sizes most of the formula's prediction for it comes from t>8, where the optimal bound collapses toward the forced choice at t=10 and the rules, which ignore the horizon, don't. There, the gap between the bounds is not small, so the formula, a statement about small gaps, need not hold, and that part of the prediction comes from comparing rules that ignore the horizon with a finite-horizon optimum.

  10. What is an “effective order,” and how does a growth factor of 4 per doubling give order 2?

    Worked answer

    If the loss is about Kap, then log⁡(L(a2)/L(a1))/log⁡(a2/a1)=p; that ratio, measured, is the effective order. A doubling of a that multiplies the loss by 4 gives p=log⁡4/log⁡2=2.

  11. Why aren't the weighted rule's losses at the smallest amplitudes used for its order?

    Worked answer

    At a=0.05, the two grids disagree even in sign (−1.4×10−6 against 9.9×10−8): in the words of the table's caption, the loss is below what the grid resolves. At a=0.1, the two values are close (6.2×10−7 and 6.4×10−7), but both are tiny, and the paper estimates the effective order only from a=0.2 to 0.8, where it reports that the grids agree to within 29% or better.

Common misreadings.

  • “The weighted rule recovers nearly all of the loss on every schedule.”The development figures are shares of each family's total loss. Schedule by schedule, the share varies: on the 180 held-out schedules where the per-moment rule loses at least 0.01, its 10th percentile is 90.4% and its minimum 55.8%.
  • “The weighted rule always recovers at least nine-tenths of the loss.”Over the 180 held-out schedules where the per-moment rule loses at least 0.01, 90.4% is the 10th percentile; the minimum is 55.8%, and piecewise schedules are the hardest.
  • “Shifting the per-moment rule by the mean look-ahead captures the result.”On 83 of the 180 schedules without a deadline, it is worse than no shift; the kernel's shape matters.
  • “The held-out test makes the rule proved for large changes.”It is a measurement on seven families. Large changes stay outside the theorems.
  • “The look-ahead barely matters.”It barely matters in reward on typical schedules. It matters for the bound's timing, which is what the paper's prediction is about.
  • “Table 8 confirms the conjecture.”It supports it for the per-moment rule at moderate sizes. It doesn't prove it, and it is weak evidence about the weighted rule.
  • “The weighted rule's loss is fourth order.”Effective orders of 3.7 and 3.9 over a from 0.2 to 0.8 are consistent with four; the paper calls it a measurement over a short range.
