Theorem 7 says that, to first order, the best bound at each moment is the constant-cost bound for a weighted average of the coming cost. That suggests two rules that need no dynamic program: average the cost with the kernel and take its bound, or average the per-moment bounds themselves. Both are exact to first order. They differ for large changes, and before a deadline only the second gives a bound above zero. The paper's rule averages the per-moment bounds ahead with the kernel for its own level: exact to first order near a constant cost, and a heuristic beyond. Chapters 9 and 10 judge it by measurement.

Two cautions hold on every page. The rule's equation defines the bound in terms of itself, and the experiments use the output of 30 damped updates, not a chosen solution. And the rule is not faster than the dynamic program on the paper's code; what it offers is an explanation of how far ahead the bound looks, and with what weights.

Two ways to use the weights

Theorem 7 says that, for a small change in the cost, the best bound at time t is to first order the constant-cost bound evaluated at a weighted average of the coming cost, with the kernel w as the weights. Read as a recipe, that gives two rules, and neither needs a dynamic program.

The first averages the cost, then takes its bound:

bavg(t)=B((wc(t)⋆c)(t)),

where wc is the kernel for the constant cost c, here the current cost. The second averages the per-moment bounds themselves:

bw(t)=∫0∞wbw(t)(s)B(c(t+s))ds,

where wb is the kernel computed for the interval (−b,b) and B(∞)=0. This one uses the kernel for its own level, and it is the paper's weighted look-ahead rule. For a small change, both rules are exact to first order (see the section on Proposition 13 below). For a large change, they differ, and a deadline is the extreme case.

The plate draws both on your schedule, over the best bound from the dynamic program, with each rule's loss. Before you add a deadline, guess what the averaged-cost rule will do.

Past a deadline T, the cost is infinite, and B(∞)=0. The kernel is positive at every delay (Lemma 6(a)), so at any time before T the weighted average of the cost gives infinite values positive weight and is itself infinite. By its definition, the averaged-cost rule's bound is then 0 from the start, which means deciding at once on whatever evidence there is. Averaging bounds instead puts the weight past the deadline on B(∞)=0 and the rest on finite bounds. For a cost c0 up to the deadline and t<T,

bw(t)=B(c0)∫0T−twbw(t)(s)ds,

the constant-cost bound times the share of the rule's own kernel that falls before the deadline. As t nears T, that share shrinks and the bound comes down. At T the choice is forced, and past T every cost ahead is infinite and the bound is 0; the paper writes this as bw=0 at and after a deadline. No special case is needed.

The paper's code computes the averaged-cost rule with kernels that end at 14/λ1 (the bins of Algorithm 1), leaving out less than one part in a million of each kernel's weight. It builds each time's kernel for that time's cost, so how far the kernel reaches changes with the cost, and it reads the costs only at the kernel's 240 bin midpoints. At a time when no midpoint falls past the deadline, the code averages only finite costs and gives a finite bound, even if the last bin's far edge reaches past the deadline; when one does, the code gives 0. On a schedule whose cost changes, the code's bound can be 0 at first, finite for a while, and 0 again. The plate draws the rule as the paper defines it, and, where the code's version differs on your schedule, draws that too, with its loss. The losses the paper measures for this rule come from its code.

A large rise without a deadline shows the same difference in a milder form. Both rules give bounds of at least 0, but they weigh a costly stretch differently. Averaged as bounds, a costly stretch can lower each moment's contribution only toward 0, so its effect is at most its weight times the bound it replaces. Averaged as costs, the same stretch adds its weight times its cost, which has no limit, and as that average grows without limit the bound it gives approaches 0, however short the stretch. For any finite cost the bound stays above 0; only an infinite cost, as past a deadline, gives exactly 0. On the cost that jumps 16-fold, the averaged-cost rule sits far below the best bound well before the jump.

The averaged-cost rule's 0 before a deadline follows exactly from its definition and Lemma 6(a). What no theorem covers is how well either rule does on a deadline or after a large change. Theorem 7 and Proposition 13 are about small changes in a continuous cost; beyond them, the paper treats both rules as heuristics and judges them by measurement. On the held-out schedules of chapter 10, averaging costs beat the per-moment rule on 169 of the 180 without a deadline and lost up to 42.64 points per 100 trials on deadlines; the weighted rule lost at most 0.235 on any of them.

Definition 12 (Weighted look-ahead).

A bound defined by itself

The weighted rule's equation has bw(t) on both sides: the level determines the kernel, and the kernel's average of the per-moment bounds gives the level. The paper solves it by damped fixed-point iteration (Algorithm 1): start from the per-moment bound, average the per-moment bounds ahead with the kernel for the current level, move halfway from the current bound to that average, and do this 30 times. Each time point is solved on its own; the times are linked only through the cost ahead.

The plate runs the 30 updates on your schedule. Guess first: how many updates until no time in the window moves by more than 0.001 in an update?

Where the change is small or slow, the average hardly depends on the level, so each update closes about half of the distance that remains, and the bound settles well within the 30 updates. The moves don't fall all the way to zero, though: the kernel's level is rounded to 0.001, so a time whose level sits near a rounding edge can step back and forth between two kernels by a small amount for as long as the updates run.

On Figure 1(d)'s deadline, Algorithm 1 doesn't settle at some times, 0.70 among them, for a reason that lies in its bins rather than in the equation; at others, such as 0.94, it does. Before the deadline, the per-moment bounds ahead are B(c0) up to the deadline and 0 past it, and the algorithm reads them at its 240 bin midpoints. As the level changes, the bins move, and when a midpoint crosses the deadline, that bin's whole weight drops out of the average at once. Where such a jump falls near the level the updates are heading for, as at 0.70, they step back and forth across it. Integrated exactly up to the deadline, the average changes smoothly with the level, and the damped updates settle there; the plate in the section on two answers draws that map for your schedule. The plate above shows where the moves stay large on your schedule. For some large, fast changes, such as the pulse, the updates settle slowly: there the average rises almost as fast as the level, so each update closes only a small part of the distance left, and 30 aren't enough.

The paper states that for large and fast changes, 30 updates need not reach a solution of the equation, and that the experiments use the output of the 30th update, an implemented approximation. Its check is that 300 updates instead of 30 move the bound by at most 0.013 before t=min(6,deadline), and the loss by less than 0.0001 points per 100 trials, on the eight schedules of its grid check (Appendix C). The plate's last button runs the same comparison on your schedule; a small difference there says the two outputs are close, not that either solves the equation.

Why it is first-order exact

Proposition 13 is the rule's one guarantee. Let the cost be c0+εγ(t) with γ continuous and |γ|≤M. For small |ε|, the rule's equation has exactly one solution at each time in the interval I=[B(c0+|ε|M),B(c0−|ε|M)], and

bw(t)=b0+εβ(t)+O(ε2)

uniformly in t, with β=B′(c0)(w⋆γ), the same first-order term as the best bound's in Theorem 7. So, against the best bound, the weighted rule's error divided by ε goes to 0, uniformly over bounded stretches of time, while the per-moment rule's tends to B′(c0)(γ−w⋆γ), which is not identically zero unless γ is constant. If γ is also Lipschitz, the weighted rule is within O(ε2) of the best bound, uniformly in time. These are statements about the equation's exact solution; the plate computes Algorithm 1's output, with its rounding, its bins, and its 30 updates, at four sizes of change.

The key step is that every kernel integrates to one. Swapping one kernel for a nearby one changes the average of a constant by nothing, so it changes the average of a nearly constant function, such as the per-moment bounds under a small change, only at second order. That makes the right side of the equation nearly flat in the level: a contraction. The proof is six steps. Scrub through them; the plate beside each step draws what the step is about, for c(t)=0.1(1+εsin⁡2t).

The full proof, from Appendix A

Let I=[B(c0+|ε|M),B(c0−|ε|M)] and Tε(b;t)=∫0∞wb(s)B(c(t+s))ds for b∈I. Since B is decreasing and each wb is a probability density, Tε(⋅;t) maps I into I. The kernel for level b is the series (16) with b in place of b0, divided by its total mass tanh⁡b+bsech2b, which is positive and smooth in b. Its terms qj(b,s)=(2/b)[κj2/(1+κj2)]e−(1+κj2)s/2, with κj=(j+12)π/b, have ∂bqj=(qj/b)[sκj2−1−2/(1+κj2)], and on a compact set of levels both ∥qj∥L1 and ∥∂bqj∥L1 are at most a constant times (j+12)−2. So b↦wb is continuously differentiable into L1 on compact subsets of (0,∞), with ∥∂bwb∥L1 bounded on I, and ∫∂bwb=0 because each wb integrates to one. Hence

(39)|∂bTε(b;t)|=|∫∂bwb(s)[B(c(t+s))−b0]ds|≤∥∂bwb∥L1supu|B(c(u))−b0|=O(|ε|),

so Tε(⋅;t) is a contraction on I for small |ε|, with a unique fixed point. Expanding B(c(t+s))=b0+εB′(c0)γ(t+s)+O(ε2) and using |bw(t)−b0|=O(ε) and the Lipschitz dependence of wb on b gives bw(t)=b0+εB′(c0)(wb0⋆γ)(t)+O(ε2) uniformly. The statements about (bw−bε)/ε and (bpm−bε)/ε follow from Theorem 7, and so does bw−bε=O(ε2) for a Lipschitz γ. The same L1 bound shows that bavg is exact to first order: wc(t) is the kernel for the level B(c(t))=b0+O(ε), so ∥wc(t)−wc0∥L1=O(ε), and since both kernels integrate to one, (wc(t)⋆c)(t)−(wc0⋆c)(t)=∫(wc(t)−wc0)(s)[c(t+s)−c0]ds=O(ε2), so bavg=b0+εβ+O(ε2).

Finally, if γ=w⋆γ for a bounded continuous γ, then γ is constant. Extend γ to the whole line: for x<0, let Γ(x)=E[γ(x+SN)], where Sn is a sum of n independent steps with density w and N is the first n with x+Sn≥0, and let Γ=γ on [0,∞). Since the steps are positive, N is finite, and conditioning on the first step gives Γ=w⋆Γ on the whole line, with Γ bounded. The kernel has exponential tails, so its Fourier transform is smooth, and the distributional Fourier transform of Γ is supported where w~(−iξ)=1, which by Corollary 8 is only ξ=0. So Γ is a polynomial and, being bounded, a constant.

What the proposition covers, and what it doesn't. Small changes of a continuous cost, bounded between two positive constants, around a constant, on an infinite horizon. It is the only guarantee the rule has. It says nothing about large steps, deadlines, or the forced choice at t=10 in the experiments: there the rule is a heuristic motivated by the theorem, and chapters 9 and 10 measure it. The same argument shows that the averaged-cost rule is exact to first order too, so first-order exactness doesn't separate the two rules; large changes do.

When there are two answers

For a large change, the contraction argument fails, and the equation can have more than one solution. Take a cost that is high for a short time and then low. A small level has a short kernel, which lies on the expensive stretch, where the per-moment bounds are small, so a small level can average back to itself. A large level has a longer kernel, which lies mostly on the cheap stretch that follows, where the per-moment bounds are large, so a large level can average back to itself too.

What it costs to compute

Algorithm 1 is the rule as the experiments compute it. Start from the per-moment bound. Thirty times: at each grid time, round the current level to the nearest 0.001 (using 0.01 for anything below it), look up that level's kernel (built once from 4000 terms of its series, integrated over 240 bins and renormalized, each bin placed at its midpoint, then cached), average the per-moment bounds at those 240 future times with it, and move halfway from the current bound to that average. Finally, move the bound inward by 0.5826Δt, not below 0, because the scorer checks the evidence only every Δt. The constant-cost bound comes from a 2001-point table in log⁡c on [10−5,104], with finite costs clipped to that range and B(∞)=0.

Exercises

Worked answers are folded under each question. Nothing is gated.

  1. Why does averaging costs fail on a deadline, when the theorem says it is right to first order?

    Worked answer

    The theorem is about small changes. A deadline makes the cost infinite past T. The kernel is positive at every delay (Lemma 6(a)), so the weighted average of a cost that is infinite past T is infinite, and B(∞)=0: the rule's bound is zero, which means deciding at once on whatever evidence there is. The paper's summary is the sentence in Section 5: it fails when the cost becomes infinite. The measured result is the 42.64-point loss.

  2. The cost is c0 until a deadline T. Write the rule's equation for t<T, and say what it does as t→T.

    Worked answer

    B(c(t+s)) is B(c0) for s<T−t and B(∞)=0 after, so bw(t)=B(c0)∫0T−twbw(t)(s)ds: the constant-cost bound times the chance that a decision ends before the deadline, when it starts from the start law for the rule's own level and stops at that level (Lemma 6(b)). The level and its kernel change together, so the equation defines the bound at each t implicitly; the experiments approximate it with the 30-update iteration, which need not reach a solution for large, fast changes. At T the choice is forced, and past T the bound is 0 (the paper writes bw=0 at and after a deadline). Before T, the paper reports what it computes, not a theorem: the bound comes down smoothly toward the deadline (Figure 1(d)).

  3. Why average bounds rather than costs, if both are first-order exact?

    Worked answer

    They differ for large changes. A cost spike can pull the optimal bound down only to zero. In an average of bounds, a huge cost for a moment contributes a bound near zero for that moment, so the most it can remove is that moment's weight times the bound it replaces. In an average of costs, it contributes its weight times the huge cost, which can dominate the average and push the bound close to zero; only an infinite cost gives exactly zero. The deadline is the extreme case, as the first section of this chapter shows.

  4. Why the kernel for the rule's own level, and not for the current cost?

    Worked answer

    Near a constant cost, either choice gives the same first-order answer, because changing the kernel moves the average only at second order (Proposition 13). For large changes, the level-matched kernel makes the rule self-consistent: its weights are the law of the decision time for a bound at that level, from that level's start law (Lemma 6(b)). The held-out schedules measure what the iteration adds: a single pass with the kernel for the per-moment bound loses up to 1.29 points per 100 trials, against 0.235 for the iterated rule (chapter 10).

  5. Why does “every wb integrates to one” make the choice of kernel a second-order effect?

    Worked answer

    Split the per-moment bounds into their constant part and the rest: B(c(t+s))=b0+[B(c(t+s))−b0]. Changing the kernel from wb0 to wb changes the average of the constant part by b0∫(wb−wb0)=0. It changes the average of the rest by at most ∥wb−wb0∥L1sup|B(c)−b0|, and both factors are O(ε) (the level is within O(ε) of b0, and so is every per-moment bound). The product is O(ε2).

  6. How can the equation have two solutions for a large change?

    Worked answer

    Take a cost that is high for a short time and then low. A small level has a short kernel, which puts most of its weight on the high cost; the per-moment bounds it averages are small, so a small level can be self-consistent. A large level has a longer kernel, which puts most of its weight on the low cost that follows; the bounds it averages are large, so a large level can be self-consistent too. The proposition's contraction argument needs the change to be small and doesn't cover this case.

  7. Why move halfway each time instead of all the way to the average?

    Worked answer

    Damping, an implementation choice: moving halfway, b←12(b+a), is a common way to keep a fixed-point iteration from overshooting when the map is steep. It isn't a guarantee: Section 5 says that for large and fast changes, 30 updates need not reach a solution. The paper's check is that 300 updates instead of 30 move the bound by at most 0.013 before t=min(6,deadline), and the loss over the whole trial by less than 0.0001 points per 100 trials.

  8. What is the final inward shift for, and why does the dynamic program not need it?

    Worked answer

    The rule's bound is a continuous-monitoring bound, but the scorer checks the evidence only every Δt, which lets paths overshoot. To leading order, the optimal discretely checked boundary sits 0.5826σΔt inside the continuous one (chapter 9). The dynamic program is solved on the scorer's own grid, so it already accounts for the discreteness.

  9. Why isn't a rule built from a closed-form kernel faster than backward induction?

    Worked answer

    At every time point and in each of 30 updates, it computes a weighted sum over 240 future times, and it builds a kernel from a 4000-term series for each new level it meets. The dynamic program on the evidence grid is a sequence of convolutions done with the fast Fourier transform, which is already quick on this code. The paper claims no faster way to compute the rule.

Common misreadings.

  • “Averaging the cost is the paper's rule.”The paper's rule averages bounds (the paper's definition, equation (25)). Averaging costs is the first rule the theorem suggests, and the paper reports it as a comparison.
  • “The weighted look-ahead is the optimal rule.”It is first-order exact near a constant cost (Proposition 13) and a heuristic beyond that, judged by measurement.
  • “The rule predicts the future cost.”The cost schedule is known in advance; the rule only weights it. Nothing is forecast.
  • “The experiments solve the rule's equation.”They run 30 damped updates from the per-moment bound. For large and fast changes, that need not reach a solution; 300 updates instead of 30 move the bound by at most 0.013 before t=min(6,deadline), and the loss by less than 0.0001 points per 100 trials (Appendix C).
  • “Proposition 13 covers deadlines.”It covers small changes in a continuous bounded cost. On deadlines, the rule is a heuristic.
  • “The kernel is used exactly.”It is binned: 240 bins on a geometric grid, with the first bin [0,10−5] holding a quarter of the mass at the smallest level (a typed detail of the algorithm's note).
  • “The weighted look-ahead is a fast or cheap approximation.”The paper says it is not faster than the dynamic program on its code. What it offers is an explanation, not speed.
