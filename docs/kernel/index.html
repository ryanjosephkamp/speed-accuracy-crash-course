For a small change in the cost of time, the best bound moves in proportion to a weighted average of the changes still to come. This chapter finds the weights, describes them two ways, derives their Laplace transform, and proves the theorem that puts them to work. It is the center of the paper: every later chapter uses it.

One caution runs through every page. That a small change moves the bound by a weighted average of the future change (a convolution) is not new: specializing Stojanovic's (1997) perturbation formula to this model at a constant cost formally gives the same form, with the survival flux as the weights. What the paper claims, to its knowledge, is the kernel made explicit, its transform, and the proof for this model on an infinite horizon.

Small changes, scaled up

Chapter 5 gave an exact equation for the best bound, but no formula for it. This chapter gets a formula by asking a smaller question: what does the bound do when the cost moves only a little away from a constant?

Write the cost as c(t)=c0+εγ(t): a constant c0, a shape γ that says how the cost moves, and a size ε. At ε=0, the bound is the flat b0=B(c0) of chapter 3. For a small ε, it moves away from b0 by roughly ε times something, so dividing the move by ε gives a curve that should settle down as ε shrinks. The theorem says what it settles to: β(t)=B′(c0)(w⋆γ)(t), the slope of the constant-cost bound times a weighted average of the coming values of γ.

The plate shows it happening on a grid. The gray curves come from the same kind of dynamic program the paper uses as its reference, on a coarser grid, measured from that program's own bound at the constant cost, which cancels the offsets the two runs share. As the change shrinks from 80% of the cost to 10%, they come closer and closer to the green curve. The dashed curve is what the per-moment rule would say, all the weight on the present: it has no lead, and the gap to it stays: for this sinusoid, the per-moment curve divided by ε tends to a different sinusoid from β.

What the plate can't show is the limit itself. A grid has a finest resolution, and the theorem is about the continuous model; the page shows what the grid gives. Chapter 9 tests the same thing at more costs and frequencies.

The paper's statement, in its introduction

The result. Write the cost as a small perturbation of a constant, c(t)=c0+εγ(t), and let b0=B(c0). We show that

(2)b(t)=b0+εB′(c0)∫0∞w(s)γ(t+s)ds+o(ε),

locally uniformly in t, where w is an explicit probability density on (0,∞). So, to first order, the optimal bound at time t is the constant-cost bound evaluated at a weighted average of the future cost. The per-moment rule puts all the weight at s=0, so its error is of order ε unless the cost is constant; it is right only in the limit of very slow changes. In reward, though, it is already close to optimal: an error of order ε in the bound costs reward only of order ε2 (Proposition 10), and on our held-out schedules it loses a median of 0.16% of the reward. The look-ahead matters most for where the bound sits and when it moves.

Where the weights come from

Before the definition, a guess. If the cost is about to change, how much should the change at each future moment count toward where the bound sits now? Draw it.

Fix the constant cost c0 and its bound b0=B(c0), and let τ0 be the first time Y reaches b0 (equivalently, the first time X leaves (−b0,b0)). The chance Px(τ0>s) of still being undecided at time s is 0 at the bound and positive inside it. Its slope at the bound, n(s)=−∂xPx(τ0>s) at x=b0, is the survival flux; normalized to integrate to 1, it is the kernel w:

n(s)=−∂xPx(τ0>s)|x=b0,
w(s)=n(s)∫0∞n(u)du.

The weighted average of a future change is (w⋆γ)(t)=∫0∞w(s)γ(t+s)ds: it looks only forward, from t. The next plate shows where the flux comes from: the surface of "still undecided" chances, and its slope at the bound.

The paper's definition

Let τ0 be the first time Y reaches b0=B(c0), which is the first exit time of X from (−b0,b0). Define the survival flux at the bound and its normalization,

(14)n(s)=−∂xPx(τ0>s)|x=b0,w(s)=n(s)∫0∞n(u)du.

For a bounded function γ, write

(15)(w⋆γ)(t)=∫0∞w(s)γ(t+s)ds.

The paper's source, version 1.2, main.tex lines 393–405

Two things to notice. The slope is steep early, because a decision that starts at the bound's edge ends almost at once, and the flux behaves like s−1/2 near 0 (chapter 7). And it never changes sign: the chance of still being undecided only falls as the start moves toward the bound, so n(s)≥0, and Lemma 6(a) shows it is strictly positive.

The same weights, a second way

The flux is one description of the weights. Lemma 6 gives a second, and it explains their shape. The kernel is the distribution of the decision time τ0 when the starting evidence is drawn with density π0(y)=4c0cosh2⁡y on [0,b0], a law that rises steeply toward the bound:

w(s)ds=∫0b0π0(y)Py(τ0∈ds)dy.

Where do you think the starts that matter sit?

cosh2⁡y grows like e2y, so most of the starting weight lies within about one unit below the bound, and decisions that start there end soon. That is why the kernel is short. At c0=0.1, the bound looks ahead 0.546 time units on average, while a decision from 0 takes 1.17. As c0→0, the distance b0−Y0 becomes exponential with rate 2, the drift near the bound is nearly 1, and the expected time to cover that distance tends to 12.

The transform, derived

The third part of Lemma 6 is a closed form for the kernel's Laplace transform:

w~(λ)=2c0cosh2⁡b0[rtanh⁡(rb0)−tanh⁡b0]λ,r=2λ+1.

Most of chapter 7 (the transfer function, the limits of the look-ahead, the half-integration) comes from this one line. The proof is five steps. Scrub through them; the plate beside each step draws what that step is about.

Scrub the proof

Step 1 of 5

Undo the drift: the evidence is a Brownian motion, reweighted.

X is a Brownian motion h-transformed by h(s,x)=e−s/2cosh⁡x, so Px(τ0>s)=e−s/2cosh⁡xEx[cosh⁡Ws; τW>s] for a Brownian motion W that must stay in (−b,b).

Step 2 of 5

Expand in waves that vanish at the bound, and take the slope there.

Expand cosh⁡x in the even Dirichlet eigenfunctions cos⁡(κjx) on (−b,b), κj=(j+12)π/b, and differentiate at x=b: every term of the series (16) is positive.

Step 3 of 5

The transform of the decision time solves a second-order equation.

For the transform, u(x)=Exe−λτ0 solves 12u″+tanh⁡(x)u′=λu with u(±b)=1, so u=χ(x)/χ(b) with χ(x)=cosh⁡(rx)/cosh⁡x and r=2λ+1.

Step 4 of 5

Integrate over time to get the flux's transform, and normalize.

Since ∫0∞e−λsPx(τ0>s)ds=(1−u(x))/λ, the flux's transform is n~(λ)=χ′(b)/(λχ(b))=[rtanh⁡(rb)−tanh⁡b]/λ, and n~(0)=tanh⁡b+bsech2b=1/(2c0cosh2⁡b) by the constant-cost equation. Normalizing gives (a) and (c).

Step 5 of 5

Average over the start law: the same transform, so the same law.

For (b), integrate Eye−λτ0 against π0(y)=4c0cosh2⁡y: the result is 4c0cosh2⁡b[rtanh⁡(rb)−tanh⁡b]/(r2−1), which is w~(λ) because r2−1=2λ. Two probability measures with the same Laplace transform are equal.

The theorem, step by step

Let the cost be c0+εγ(t), with γ continuous and bounded. Then, as ε→0, the optimal bound is the constant-cost bound plus εB′(c0) times the kernel-weighted average of the coming values of γ:

bε(t)=b0+εB′(c0)(w⋆γ)(t)+o(ε).

To first order, then, the optimal bound at time t is the constant-cost bound evaluated at a weighted average of the future cost. The limit is uniform over bounded time intervals, and over all times if γ is uniformly continuous. If γ is Hölder continuous with exponent α, the remainder is O(|ε|1+α) uniformly in time, so O(ε2) when γ is Lipschitz.

The proof uses the exact equation of chapter 5, which has no derivative of the bound in it. That is what makes it short: subtract two exact equations, bracket one decision time between two others, and divide by ε.

Scrub the proof

Step 1 of 6

Subtract two exact equations: one for the changed cost, one for the constant.

With c0>0 and γ bounded, both cε=c0+εγ and c0 stay between two positive constants for small ε. Apply the exact equation to each, and subtract. With F(b)=∫0bcosh2: c0[F(bε(t))−F(b0)]=−εIε(t), where Iε(t)=∫0bε(t)cosh2⁡yEy[γ(t+τt,yε)]dy.

Step 2 of 6

The changed bound stays within a band of width proportional to the change.

By Lemma 1(c) and the smoothness of B, |bε−b0|≤a|ε| for a constant a.

Step 3 of 6

So two fixed levels bracket the decision time, and both close on τ0.

For starts below b0−a|ε|, the decision time τε lies between the hitting times of the fixed levels b0±a|ε|. Both converge almost surely to τ0, the hitting time of b0.

Step 4 of 6

In the limit, the integral is the kernel-weighted average.

γ is uniformly continuous on compact sets and the bracketing times don't depend on t, so, on the common coupling, almost surely supt≤T|γ(t+τε)−γ(t+τ0)|→0; the thin strip near the bound adds O(|ε|). Dominated convergence gives Iε→I0=∫0b0cosh2⁡yEy[γ(t+τ0)]dy=(w⋆γ)(t)/(4c0), by Lemma 6(b).

Step 5 of 6

Divide by ε and read off β.

The left side is c0cosh2⁡(ξ)(bε−b0) for some ξ between b0 and bε (mean value theorem). Divide by ε: (bε−b0)/ε→−(w⋆γ)/(4c02cosh2⁡b0)=B′(c0)(w⋆γ), uniformly on [0,T].

Step 6 of 6

If the change is Hölder, the remainder has a rate.

If |γ(u)−γ(v)|≤L|u−v|α, Jensen's inequality bounds |Eγ(t+τε)−Eγ(t+τ0)| by L[E(σ+−σ−)]α, and E(σ+−σ−)=O(|ε|) from Eyσz=ztanh⁡z−ytanh⁡y. So the error in step 4's limit is O(|ε|α) for starts below the band; the thin strip near the bound adds O(|ε|), and the mean-value denominator of step 5 changes by O(|ε|). The bound's remainder is O(|ε|1+α).

What the theorem covers, and what it doesn't. Small, continuous, bounded changes of the cost around a constant. It says nothing about large changes, discontinuous steps, or deadlines, and nothing about the weighted look-ahead rule (that is Proposition 13, in chapter 8). The per-moment rule would put all the weight at s=0.

On your schedule

Theorem 7, with Proposition 13, compares two shortcuts. The per-moment rule's error in the bound shrinks no faster than the size of the change: divided by ε, it tends to B′(c0)(γ−w⋆γ), which is not identically zero unless γ is constant. With the first-order correction, the error shrinks faster than the change, o(ε); when the change is smooth (Lipschitz), it is O(ε2).

Here is that comparison on the schedule you chose on the contents page. The page writes your schedule as c0+εγ, with c0 its average over the window, and shrinks the change. The program adds numerical errors of its own (from the grid, the finite horizon, and the kernel's series), which can change with ε, so the page compares the two errors at each size instead of reading the theory's rates off the grid. For a schedule the theorem covers, a bounded change that moves at all (any schedule except a deadline, the rising cost, and a cost that doesn't change, such as Constant, Smooth at size 0, or a flat drawing), the plate asks how fast the theory says each error shrinks before it draws the bottom chart. The theory's limit for the per-moment rule is a function on all times t≥0; the plate measures the errors only at the times from 0 to 2.5, every 0.02.

Two cautions about the bottom chart. The guide lines are guides only: for a Lipschitz change the theorem bounds the corrected error by a constant times ε2, which doesn't fix the slope you see at these sizes. And the rising cost, which grows without bound, is outside the theorem: the page computes it and shows how the two errors compare, with no theory question. A deadline isn't a small change of a constant cost, and the plate doesn't run on it.

Exercises

Worked answers are folded under each question. Nothing is gated.

  1. Why is n(s)≥0?

    Worked answer

    At x=b0 the evidence is already at the bound, so Pb0(τ0>s)=0; just inside the bound the probability is positive. So Px(τ0>s) decreases toward the bound, and its derivative at x=b0 is ≤0, making n(s)=−∂xPx(τ0>s)|x=b0≥0. Lemma 6(a) shows it is strictly positive, as a sum of positive exponentials.

  2. Let γ be 0 before time T and 1 after it. What is (w⋆γ)(t) for t<T, in words?

    Worked answer

    (w⋆γ)(t)=∫T−t∞w(s)ds, the probability that a decision time with density w exceeds T−t. By Lemma 6(b): the chance that a decision started at time t from the law π0 is still open when the change arrives. For the ideal step, this is a calculation with the kernel (Theorem 7 needs a continuous γ). A continuous logistic step has its own kernel-averaged value, (w⋆γ)(t) computed from its actual shape, which approaches this tail as the step's width shrinks; for a fixed width, use the logistic itself.

  3. Check that π0 integrates to 1 and that w~(0)=1.

    Worked answer

    ∫0b04c0cosh2⁡ydy=4c0(12b0+14sinh⁡2b0)=c0(2b0+sinh⁡2b0)=1 by the constant-cost equation. For the transform, w~=n~⋅2c0cosh2⁡b0 (normalizing by ∫n=1/(2c0cosh2⁡b0)), so w~(0)=n~(0)⋅2c0cosh2⁡b0=1.

  4. Show that u(x)=cosh⁡(rx)/cosh⁡x solves 12u″+tanh⁡(x)u′=λu when r2=2λ+1.

    Worked answer

    The generator factors: 12u″+tanh⁡xu′=1cosh⁡x(12∂x2−12)(cosh⁡x⋅u). (Expand: 12(cosh⁡xu)″−12cosh⁡xu=sinh⁡xu′+12cosh⁡xu″; divide by cosh⁡x.) So the equation becomes v″=(1+2λ)v=r2v for v=cosh⁡x⋅u, whose even solution is v=cosh⁡(rx). This factoring is the h-transform of step 1 in one line.

  5. Use the second description to explain, without computing, why the mean look-ahead stays near 12 when time is nearly free, even though the mean decision time grows without bound.

    Worked answer

    When c0 is small, b0 is large, and π0∝cosh2⁡y≈e2y/4 puts almost all its weight within a unit or so of the bound: the distance b0−Y0 is close to exponential with rate 2, so its mean is about 12. Near the bound, the drift tanh⁡y is nearly 1, so covering that distance takes about 12 a time unit on average. The typical decision starts at 0, far from the bound, and takes b0tanh⁡b0, which grows without bound; but those starts carry almost no weight in π0. Corollary 9(b) makes this exact.

  6. Check the theorem for a constant change, γ≡γ0.

    Worked answer

    w integrates to 1, so (w⋆γ)(t)=γ0 and β=B′(c0)γ0. The theorem then says bε=B(c0)+εB′(c0)γ0+o(ε), which is the first-order Taylor expansion of the exact answer B(c0+εγ0).

  7. Show that ∫0b0cosh2⁡yEy[γ(t+τ0)]dy=(w⋆γ)(t)/(4c0).

    Worked answer

    Write cosh2⁡y=π0(y)/(4c0). Then the integral is 14c0∫0b0π0(y)Ey[γ(t+τ0)]dy, and by Lemma 6(b) the start law π0 turns τ0's distribution into w: ∫0b0π0(y)Ey[γ(t+τ0)]dy=∫0∞w(s)γ(t+s)ds.

  8. Where does the proof need the exact equation's lack of a derivative of the bound?

    Worked answer

    In step 1. Subtracting two exact equations gives an identity between F(bε)−F(b0) and an integral, with no b′ anywhere, so dividing by ε and passing to the limit needs only the bracketing of step 3, not any smoothness of bε in time (which isn't known: Proposition 3 gives only Hölder continuity).

  9. The theorem is “locally uniform” in t. What would go wrong over all of [0,∞) without uniform continuity of γ?

    Worked answer

    Step 4 needs γ(t+τε)−γ(t+τ0)→0 uniformly in t. On a bounded interval, that follows from uniform continuity on compact sets. Over all times, a continuous γ could oscillate faster and faster (for example sin⁡(t2)), so a tiny shift between τε and τ0 could change γ by a fixed amount at late enough times. Uniform continuity on [0,∞) rules that out, and the paper states the uniform version under exactly that condition.

  10. For γ(t)=sin⁡ωt with ω>0, show that the per-moment error divided by ε does not tend to the zero function.

    Worked answer

    It tends to B′(c0)(γ−w⋆γ). Write γ=Im(eiωt); then (w⋆γ)(t)=Im(w~(−iω)eiωt), because ∫0∞w(s)eiω(t+s)ds=eiωtw~(−iω). Corollary 8 shows |w~(−iω)|<1, so w~(−iω)≠1, and γ−w⋆γ=Im((1−w~(−iω))eiωt) is a sinusoid of amplitude |1−w~(−iω)|>0. Multiplied by B′(c0)≠0, it is not 0.

Common misreadings.

  • “The kernel depends on the schedule.”In Theorem 7, it is fixed by the constant cost c0 alone. The weighted look-ahead rule of chapter 8 uses the kernel for its own level at each moment, which is a different object.
  • “The kernel is the distribution of decision times.”Of decision times from the special start law π0, which piles up near the bound, not from y=0.
  • “The series is how the kernel is defined.”The kernel is defined as the normalized survival flux (14). The series and the transform are proved properties of it.
  • “Campbell and Engelund already give the kernel.”The paper credits them with the two identities behind the exact equation, for smooth losses. The explicit kernel and its transform, computed from the exit-time law, are claimed as new to the paper's knowledge.
  • “To first order, the bound is the constant-cost bound at the average future cost, so averaging costs is exactly right.”It is right to first order in ε, for small changes, with the kernel fixed at c0. For large changes, averaging costs fails badly on deadlines, and the paper's rule averages bounds instead (chapter 8).
  • “The convolution form is the paper's discovery.”Specializing Stojanovic's (1997) perturbation formula to this model at a constant cost formally gives the first-order coefficient as −1/(2c0) times the convolution of γ's future values with the survival flux. The paper claims, to its knowledge, the kernel made explicit, its transform, and the proof for this model on an infinite horizon.
  • “Theorem 7 covers the 16-fold rise and the deadline.”Large changes, discontinuous steps, and deadlines lie outside it.
  • “The per-moment rule's error is first order, so it loses first-order reward.”Its bound error is first order; its reward loss is at most second order, by Proposition 10 (chapter 7): for a small, bounded, continuous change and a fixed start below the bound. That is why it is close in reward and wrong in timing.
  • “‘Smoothly’ in the abstract means differentiable.”The paper doesn't define it; it corresponds to the Lipschitz case of Theorem 7 and Proposition 13, where the error is O(ε2).
