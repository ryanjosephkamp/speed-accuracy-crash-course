Where the paper starts: a shortcut for a cost of time that changes, the one the author's 2025 capstone used, and a picture of what it misses. The per-moment rule moves the bound with the cost; the best bound moves well before most of a change. How far ahead should the bound look, and with what weights?

Nothing in this chapter is proved. It defines the shortcut, asks for a guess, and rebuilds the paper's first measurement, Figure 1, in the page. In reward, the shortcut is already close: on the paper's 210 held-out schedules, it gives up a median of 0.16% of the reward (chapter 10). What it gets wrong is where the bound sits and when it moves. The formal setup comes in chapters 2 and 3; this chapter needs only the idea that a higher cost of time means deciding sooner, on less evidence.

The capstone and its shortcut

This section's story comes from the author's blog post about the paper, not from the paper, which doesn't mention the capstone. In April 2025, the author presented his undergraduate research capstone at the University of Cincinnati: an implementation of Jan Drugowitsch's notes on normative solutions to the speed-accuracy trade-off in perceptual decision-making, written for the FENS-Hertie 2015 Winter School. The capstone's code handled a cost of time that changes during a decision with a shortcut, which the post calls the capstone's main contribution: at every moment, use the bound that would be best if the current cost lasted forever.

At the time, neither he nor the code could say how much that mattered. When he rechecked the code in the fall of 2026, he found that its dynamic-programming solver stopped after three iterations, that its belief grid was too coarse for its time step, and that it applied its transition matrix transposed. He rebuilt the solvers, checked them against the closed form, and then measured the shortcut against the best bound from a dynamic program. The paper reports that measurement, and a way to do better.

In the paper, the shortcut is the per-moment rule. At each moment, it uses the bound that would be best if the current cost of time lasted forever:

bpm(t)=B(c(t)),

where B(c), the best bound for a constant cost c, is the root of sinh⁡(2b)+2b=1/c (chapter 3 derives it). A higher cost gives a lower bound: deciding sooner, on less evidence. The paper says the rule comes from treating the problem as quasi-static. It is exact when the cost is constant, and frozen-cost bounds of this kind are the leading term in large-time asymptotics for related stopping problems with slowly changing costs (Irle, Kubillus, and Paulsen 2001; Muravlev 2020). But it ignores the future.

The plate takes your schedule's cost at each moment and applies B to it.

The paper's words, in its introduction

This paper is about the last case: a cost of time c(t) that changes in a known way. The cost can rise because waiting means losing an opportunity, braking harder, or missing a window. It can fall once an initial rush subsides or a threat recedes. The optimal bound b(t) can then be computed by backward induction over time. That is accurate but opaque: it gives the bound at every moment, but not how far ahead the bound looks or why. A natural shortcut is to use, at each moment, the constant-cost bound for the current cost:

(1)bpm(t)=B(c(t)),

where B(c) is the optimal bound for a constant cost c. This shortcut, which we call the per-moment rule, is derived by treating the problem as quasi-static. It is exact when the cost is constant, and frozen-cost bounds of this kind are the leading term in large-time asymptotics for related stopping problems with slowly changing costs (Irle et al., 2001; Muravlev, 2020). But it ignores the future.

Reading the present cost through B is all the rule does, so its bound moves when the cost moves, never before. That is the trouble. A decision still open at time t pays the cost at every moment until it ends, not only the cost at t. If the cost is about to jump, a decision still open now may well be completed after the jump, so the best bound should already be moving toward the bound the jump will call for. In the paper, the per-moment rule is the baseline that everything else is measured against, not the contribution.

Where is the bound before the rise?

Here is the first schedule of the paper's Figure 1: a smooth step, logistic in time with scale 0.05 and centered at t=0.5, from a lower level of 0.1 to an upper level of 1.6, 16 times higher. The per-moment bound is drawn, moved in for a program that checks the evidence every 0.01 time units, so it starts just below B(0.1) and comes down with the cost. The best bound, from a dynamic program the page solves backward from a forced choice, isn't drawn yet.

The best bound at t=0.4 sits well below the per-moment bound. It began coming down long before the main part of the step: by t=0.2, when the cost is still within 4% of 0.1, the best bound has come down more than three times as far as the per-moment bound. A decision open at t=0.4 may still be open as the cost climbs toward 1.6, so it pays to stop sooner, on less evidence. The per-moment rule can't do this: at t=0.4, it uses only the cost at t=0.4.

Four schedules, three rules

Figure 1 is the paper's first measurement. It compares three bounds on four schedules: (a) the 16-fold rise from the last section; (b) a fall from 0.4 to 0.05, centered at t=0.3; (c) a cost that is flat at 0.1, then rises by 0.8 per time unit after t=0.5; and (d) a cost of 0.1 until a hard deadline at t=1, where the choice is forced. The steps in (a) and (b) are logistic in time, with scale 0.05.

Two of the bounds are the best bound, from the dynamic program, and the per-moment rule. The third is a rule that the paper's answer suggests: the weighted look-ahead rule of chapter 8, which averages the per-moment bounds ahead of each moment, with weights that chapter 6 derives. Near a constant cost, it is exact to first order (Proposition 13); for changes as large as these, and on a deadline, no theorem covers it, and the paper judges it by measurement.

Move the time line across the four panels, or let it play, and pick a panel to read it out. Your own schedule is the fifth panel.

In each of Figure 1's four panels, the per-moment bound moves with the cost, and the best bound moves well before most of the change: down before most of the rise in (a), up before most of the fall in (b), down while the cost is still flat in (c), and down toward the deadline in (d), where the per-moment bound stays flat until the choice is forced. The weighted rule follows the best bound in all four, though not exactly: on the deadline, the paper notes, its bound sits below the best one for most of the trial and above it near the deadline. Read out panel (d) near t=1 to see where the page's grid puts the crossing.

The per-moment rule. For a time-varying cost, the per-moment rule (1) uses B(c(t)) at each time. Figure 1 compares it with the optimal bound for four schedules. It reacts to a cost change only when the change arrives, while the optimal bound starts to move earlier.

Table 4 gives what each rule loses against the best bound, in points per 100 trials: the difference in expected reward, computed exactly on the paper's grid rather than by simulation (chapter 9). The per-moment rule loses the most on the fall (4.29), then the rise (0.94), the deadline (0.86), and the ramp (0.68). The weighted rule loses at most 0.038, on the deadline. What Figure 1 shows, and what it doesn't. Four schedules, measured on the paper's grid with a forced choice at t=10, or at the deadline. No small-change result covers the rules on them: Theorem 7 and Proposition 13 are about small changes of a continuous, bounded cost on an infinite horizon, and the rise, the fall, and the ramp are large changes. The rise and the fall do meet the paper's standing assumption (a continuous cost between two positive constants), so the exact equation of chapter 5 holds for these costs on an infinite horizon; the ramp, which grows without bound, and the deadline, which makes the cost infinite, don't meet it. The measurements' forced choice at t=10 is itself outside the infinite-horizon setting of the theorems. On all four, the weighted rule is a heuristic, and Figure 1 and Table 4 measure it.

Each of the four panels shows the same thing. A decision open now pays the cost of every moment it lasts into, so the best bound moves before most of the change in the cost, and a rule that uses only the present cost moves late. The weighted rule moves early because it looks ahead, and on these four schedules it loses far less than the per-moment rule (Table 4). Why that works, and how far ahead is far enough, is the rest of the book. The paper puts its question this way: how far ahead should the bound look, and with what weights?

Exercises

Worked answers are folded under each question. Nothing is gated.

  1. The cost is 0.1 until a hard deadline at t=1. What bound does the per-moment rule use at t=0.9?

    Worked answer

    B(0.1), the same flat bound it uses at t=0. At t=0.9, the current cost is still 0.1, and the rule looks only at the current cost. The cost is 0.1 at t=1 as well, so the rule's value is still B(0.1) there; only past the deadline, where the cost is infinite, is it B(∞)=0. At t=1 itself the choice is forced, whatever the bound. The optimal bound has already been coming down toward 0 well before t=1 (Figure 1(d)), because a decision still open near the deadline will be forced.

  2. When, if ever, is the per-moment rule exactly optimal?

    Worked answer

    When the cost is constant, on an infinite horizon: then bpm=B(c) is the optimal flat bound. To first order, it is also right in the limit of very slow changes. For a small, bounded, continuous change around a constant cost on an infinite horizon, the first-order term weights the coming change by the kernel, and w~(0)=1, so in the low-frequency limit that weighted average is the present value of the change (Corollary 9(a)). For any such change that isn't constant, the per-moment bound's error divided by ε tends, locally uniformly in time, to a limit that isn't identically zero, so the error doesn't shrink faster than the change (Proposition 13).

  3. The paper says frozen-cost bounds are “the leading term in large-time asymptotics” for related problems. Does that contradict the claim that the per-moment rule is wrong?

    Worked answer

    No. Irle and colleagues (2001) and Muravlev (2020) show that, for related stopping problems with slowly changing costs, the frozen-cost boundary is the leading term as t→∞. That is the per-moment idea, and it is right to leading order in those limits. The paper's point is about a different expansion: for a small, bounded, continuous change of size ε around a constant cost, on an infinite horizon and on any time scale, the per-moment bound's miss, divided by ε, tends, locally uniformly in time, to a function that isn't identically zero unless the change is constant, and the first-order correction is a weighted average over the near future. Section 8, in its paragraph on asymptotics, puts it this way: those expansions are local in time, built from the cost near t; the paper's is a perturbation on an arbitrary time scale, with a nonlocal kernel.

  4. In panel (b) the cost falls. Does the optimal bound rise or fall, and does it move before or after the change?

    Worked answer

    It rises. That the per-moment bound rises follows from B being decreasing ((10)): a cheaper cost of time gives a higher constant-cost bound. That the best bound rises, and when, is measured: on the page's grid and in the printed Figure 1(b), it rises to the new level, and about three-fifths of its rise over the window comes before the center of the fall at t=0.3. The reason is the one for a bound falling before a rise: a decision open now may still be open after the change, under the new cost. The per-moment bound moves only as the cost moves: the step is logistic (scale 0.05), so it changes mostly close to t=0.3.

  5. Read Table 4. On which schedule does the per-moment rule lose the most, and does the weighted rule's ranking follow it?

    Worked answer

    The per-moment rule loses the most on the falling cost (4.29 points per 100 trials), then the rise (0.94), the deadline (0.86), and the ramp (0.68). The weighted rule loses the most on the deadline (0.038) and between 0.008 and 0.012 on the other three, so its ranking does not follow the per-moment rule's. The paper's small-change results (Theorem 7 and Proposition 13) don't justify the weighted rule on any of the four: on all of them it is a heuristic, and Table 4 measures it.

  6. Does Theorem 7 cover the 16-fold rise?

    Worked answer

    No. The rise is continuous (logistic in time) and stays between 0.1 and 1.6, so the cost meets the standing assumption and the exact equation (Proposition 4) holds for it. But Theorem 7 is a statement about the limit of small changes, ε→0; a fixed 16-fold rise is a large change. Section 5 says so: large steps, deadlines, and the forced choice at t=10 lie outside Theorem 7 and Proposition 13, and there the rules are heuristics judged by measurement.

Common misreadings.

  • “The per-moment rule is the paper's shortcut, its contribution.”It is the baseline: the 2025 capstone's rule, by the author's post (the paper doesn't mention the capstone). The paper measures it and proves that its bound is wrong at first order. What the paper claims as new, to its knowledge, is the explicit kernel and its transform, the theorem, the weighted rule, and the measurements. Specializing Stojanovic's (1997) perturbation formula to this model at a constant cost formally gives the convolution form of the correction, and the identities behind the exact equation appear, for smooth losses, in Campbell and Engelund (2026).
  • “The per-moment rule is bad.”In reward, it is close: on the 210 held-out schedules, it gives up a median of 0.16% of the reward. What it gets wrong is where the bound sits and when it moves (In reward, the per-moment rule is already close to optimal).
  • “The curves in Figure 1 are simulations.”The optimal bound is the dynamic program's, and the losses are computed by propagating the evidence density on a grid, with no sampling (Scoring without Monte Carlo).
  • “The weighted rule matches the optimal bound on the deadline.”It loses little (0.038 points per 100 trials), but its bound is visibly off: in the paper's reading of its figure (Section 5), below the optimal one for most of the trial, then above it from about t=0.9 to the deadline, by up to about 0.1.
