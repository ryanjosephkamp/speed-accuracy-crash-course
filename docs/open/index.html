An urgency signal that follows the best bound should move before a small, known change in the cost of time, not with it; the paper argues this but has no data. This chapter covers the paper's last steps outside the mathematics: that prediction and its size for one real task, what moment-by-moment control of the bound would have to compute, what in the paper is new and what was known before, the limitations the paper states, and where its code is.

Two cautions hold on every page. The prediction is a formal consequence of the first-order theory, for small changes on a known schedule; it is not a theorem, and the paper reports no test of it. And the paper's claims of novelty are narrow: each carries “to our knowledge,” and the book quotes the paper's own sentences for them.

What an urgency signal should do

This section is about a prediction. The paper argues it formally from Theorem 7, Corollary 8, and Corollary 9, for small changes in the cost on a known schedule. It is not a theorem, and the paper has no data on it.

An urgency signal is another way to describe a bound that moves. Instead of the bound falling, the evidence is multiplied by a gain U(t) that grows, and the decision is made when the gated evidence reaches a fixed level a. Smith and Ratcliff (2022) show that the two descriptions give exactly the same decisions: a gain U(t) with a fixed boundary a decides when an ungated process reaches a/U(t). So the urgency signal that implements a bound b(t) is U(t)=a/b(t), and the one that implements the best bound is the normative urgency signal.

The paper's prediction is about how that signal moves when the cost of time changes in a known way. To first order, δU/U=−δb/b0: when the bound falls, U rises, so U rises when the cost rises. And because the best bound moves ahead of the per-moment rule's, an urgency signal that implements it should lead the cost, not track it:

  • for a small change that is slow and nearly linear, by the mean look-ahead m(c0), which is at most about 0.619 time units and about 12 when time is cheap;
  • for a small sinusoidal change, by the transfer function's phase, which tends to an eighth of a period (45∘) at high frequency, with a deviation that shrinks only like ω−1/2.

For other small changes, the first-order theory predicts the whole response, δU/U=−εB′(c0)(w⋆γ)(t)/b0, rather than a single lead. An urgency signal that followed the per-moment rule would show no lead at all: it would change at the moment the cost does.

Before the plate draws U: for a cost that ramps up slowly, over 8 time units, how far ahead of the per-moment urgency does it cross the halfway line?

On a slow ramp, U crosses the halfway line very nearly m(c0) earlier than the per-moment urgency: the kernel's average of a straight line is the line a mean look-ahead later (step 4 below). On a short ramp, it crosses less far ahead, because the kernel puts much of its weight on the next instant. On the page's computation, as the ramp shortens, the lead at the halfway line falls toward the kernel's median, which is below its mean. That is why the lead of m(c0) is stated for slow changes only.

On a sine wave, the lead is a phase. In degrees, it tends to 45∘ as the frequency grows, but not always steadily: at the plate's costs of 0.3 and 1 it dips on the way, and at 1 it rises above 45∘ and falls back below it, as chapter 7 shows. In time, it is the phase divided by ω: it starts at m(c0) for slow waves and tends to 0. On the page's computation, at the plates' costs and at frequencies sampled from 0.05 to 3,000, the lead in time falls from each sample to the next; the paper states only the limits.

The argument behind the prediction is five steps: Smith and Ratcliff's (2022) equivalence, one line of algebra, and three results of chapters 6 and 7. It holds to first order in the size of the change, for a cost that changes in a known way.

Scrub the proof

Step 1 of 5

An urgency gain with a fixed boundary decides exactly when a moving bound would.

Smith and Ratcliff (2022): U(t)X(t) reaches ±a exactly when X(t) reaches ±a/U(t). So the urgency signal that implements a bound b(t) is U(t)=a/b(t), and the one that implements the best bound is the normative urgency signal.

Step 2 of 5

To first order, the urgency moves opposite to the bound, by the same share.

With b=b0+δb, U=a/b=(a/b0)(1−δb/b0+O(δb2)), so δU/U=−δb/b0 to first order.

Step 3 of 5

The bound falls ahead of a rising cost, so the urgency rises ahead of it.

By Theorem 7, δb=εB′(c0)(w⋆γ)(t) to first order, with B′(c0)<0. So δU/U=ε|B′(c0)|b0(w⋆γ)(t): the urgency moves with the kernel's average of the coming change, the same average that moves the bound ahead of the per-moment rule.

Step 4 of 5

For a slow, nearly linear change, the lead is the mean look-ahead.

If γ(t+s)=γ(t)+γ′(t)s over the kernel's reach, then (w⋆γ)(t)=γ(t)+γ′(t)m(c0)=γ(t+m(c0)), since ∫0∞w(s)ds=1 and ∫0∞sw(s)ds=m(c0) (Corollary 9(b)). The urgency runs m(c0) ahead of the cost.

Step 5 of 5

For a sinusoid, the lead is the transfer function's phase, which tends to 45∘.

For γ(t)=Re(Γeiωt), (w⋆γ)(t)=Re(w~(−iω)Γeiωt) (Corollary 8): the urgency runs arg⁡w~(−iω) ahead of the cost, a phase between 0∘ and 90∘, with arg⁡w~(−iω)→45∘ as ω→∞ (Corollary 9(c)). In time, the lead is that phase in radians divided by ω.

The paper's words, in its discussion

What the shortcut costs. In reward, the per-moment rule is already close to optimal. On our held-out schedules, it loses a median of 0.16% of the reward, under 0.1 points per 100 trials on 87 of 210 schedules, and 1 or more on only 11, up to 4.67 on the worst. What the look-ahead changes is where the bound sits and when it moves. So its clearest test is not accuracy or reward, but the timing of an urgency signal.

A testable prediction. If an urgency signal implements the optimal bound for a cost that changes in a known way, it should lead the per-moment response to the cost, not track it. For slow changes, the lead is the mean look-ahead m(c0)σ2/μ2, which is at most about 0.619σ2/μ2 and about half of σ2/μ2 when time is cheap, σ2/μ2 being the time for the evidence to reach a signal-to-noise ratio of one. At high frequencies, the lead tends to an eighth of a period, or 45∘, with a deviation that shrinks only like ω−1/2. Smith and Ratcliff (2022) show that multiplying the evidence by an urgency gain U(t) and keeping a fixed boundary a gives exactly the same decisions as an ungated process with bound a/U(t). So the normative urgency signal is U(t)=a/b(t), and, to first order, δU/U=−δb/b0: it rises when the cost rises, and leads the cost itself by the same amounts.

What the prediction says, and what it doesn't. It says what an urgency signal that implements the best bound would do, to first order, for a small change in the cost of time on a known schedule. It doesn't say that any brain implements the best bound, and the paper reports no data on it. Large changes, discontinuous steps, and deadlines lie outside Theorem 7, which the argument rests on.

In milliseconds

The paper puts the prediction on a clock for one real task: random-dot motion at 6.4% coherence and the mean observer of Palmer, Huk, and Shadlen (2005), whose drift is 21×0.064 per second with noise variance 1 per second. One time unit, σ2/μ2, is then 554 ms. Their fitted bound is 0.95 in the paper's units; read as the best bound for a constant cost, that cost is 0.19, where the mean look-ahead is 0.39 time units.

Before the plate shows the paper's numbers: if this observer's cost of time rose slowly and steadily, how many milliseconds ahead should its urgency signal run? And for a cost that rises and falls once a second, how many degrees?

The paper's numbers: a slowly changing cost, such as a linear ramp, would be led by about 217 ms. A cost oscillating at 0.5, 1, or 2 Hz would be led by about 162, 103, or 54 ms (29°, 37°, or 39°), with a swing 0.77, 0.55, or 0.40 times the per-moment one. As the frequency rises, the lead grows in degrees and shrinks in milliseconds.

The plate computes each of these from the observer's drift and fitted bound, not from the printed values, and the check script holds each to the paper's. That matters here, because the printed values are rounded and the leads are sensitive to them. Starting from the printed bound, 0.95, gives 216 ms for the ramp instead of 217; converting the printed 29° at 0.5 Hz gives 161 ms instead of 162. The paper's figure script starts from Palmer and colleagues' fitted bound, 0.71 in their units, which the paper doesn't print: 21×0.064×0.71=0.95424 in the paper's units.

The time unit scales as 1/coherence2, since the drift is proportional to coherence: at half the coherence, one time unit is four times as long. A task in which the reward rate or a penalty for slowness changes within a trial on a known schedule could test it. Kira, Zylberberg, and Shadlen (2025) show how time-dependent bounds can be estimated from choices and reaction times alone, and Kalburge and colleagues (2026) measured bounds directly, trial by trial, in a task in which the evidence's signal-to-noise ratio can change at a cued time within a trial. The paper notes that the same design, with a change in the cost of time at the cued moment, could measure the lead directly.

What moment-by-moment control would have to compute

Smith and Ratcliff (2025, p. 163) point out that Bellman-optimal bounds must be adjusted continuously, moment by moment, and count the lack of a model of how such control is maintained as a significant weakness of the collapsing-bounds account. The paper's answer is not such a model. It says what that control would have to compute: to first order, the bound at each moment is a fixed kernel's average of the constant-cost bounds for the costs ahead, looking at most about 0.619 time units ahead on average.

The plate shows that computation. At the moment you set, the kernel for the constant cost c0 is drawn forward over the cost ahead, and the average, with those weights, of the constant-cost bounds B(c(t+s)) is the fixed-kernel approximation of the bound at that moment. To first order in the size of the change, it is the best bound; at the plate's 25% change it is an approximation.

The average reaches only a short way: half of the weight lies within a fraction of a time unit, and the mean is at most about 0.619. To first order in the size of the change, this average of bounds and Theorem 7's bound b0+εB′(c0)(w⋆γ) agree. The weighted look-ahead rule of chapter 8 does the same averaging with the kernel for its own level at each moment; on the paper's code, it is not faster than the dynamic program (chapter 8). Whether a brain's bound does this is what the prediction in this chapter's first section could test; the paper has no data on it.

What is new, and what isn't

The paper states what is new in it narrowly, and the book keeps to its words. First, a self-test: for each statement, is it credited to others, proved in the paper but not claimed as new, or new to the paper's knowledge?

Credited to others. Expanding the value-matching and smooth-fit conditions about an unperturbed free boundary is a standard formal procedure in the asymptotic analysis of American options (Fouque and colleagues). Stojanovic (1997) establishes the resulting perturbation formula in a weak sense, for regular free boundaries under perturbations of the source term: for elliptic obstacle problems on bounded domains, and for parabolic ones on finite cylinders, assuming in the parabolic case that the linearized problem has a unique solution. Here, the cost of time is that source term, and specializing his formula formally to this model at a constant cost gives the first-order coefficient of the bound as −1/(2c0) times the convolution of the future values of γ with the survival flux at the unperturbed bound. So neither the technique nor the convolution form is new. The two identities behind the exact equation (Lemma 2) appear, for smooth terminal losses, in Campbell and Engelund (2026), and in their symmetric infinite-horizon setting with smooth losses, their identities and smooth fit would also give a spatially integrated relation like the exact equation. And bounds that move with a known change are not new: Drugowitsch and colleagues (2012) computed optimal bounds for time-varying costs by backward induction, and Barendregt and colleagues (2022) find thresholds that anticipate a known drop in reward.

Proved in the paper, not claimed as new. Campbell and Engelund's hypotheses exclude the drift-diffusion model's 0-1 loss, which has a kink, so the paper proves the identities and the regularity directly, proves the exact equation (Proposition 4) for the 0-1 loss, and uses it to prove the first-order theorem. The paper lists the exact equation among its contributions, but its sentences on what is new don't claim it.

New, to the paper's knowledge. The kernel made explicit, as the density of the time to reach b0 from a start drawn with density proportional to cosh2⁡y on [0,b0], and through its Laplace transform (Lemma 6); a proof of the expansion for the drift-diffusion model on an infinite horizon, outside the finite cylinders of Stojanovic's theorem, locally uniformly, through an exact equation with no derivative of the bound, with a rate when the change is Hölder continuous (Theorem 7); the weighted look-ahead rule (Proposition 13); and the measurements. The paper proves the kernel's descriptions, the theorem, and the rule's first-order exactness too; these are both proved and claimed as new. Also to the paper's knowledge, none of the exact-solution work it reviews, which computes optimal bounds numerically or studies their structure, gives an analytic approximation of the optimal bound of a drift-diffusion model for small changes of general shape in the cost of time.

Each claim of novelty carries “to our knowledge” in the paper, and the book keeps the qualifier.

On boundary sensitivity, from its related work

Boundary sensitivity. Expanding the value-matching and smooth-fit conditions about an unperturbed free boundary is a standard formal procedure in the asymptotic analysis of American options (Fouque et al., 2001, 2011). Stojanovic (1997) establishes the resulting perturbation formula in a weak sense for regular free boundaries under perturbations of the source term. The result applies to elliptic obstacle problems on bounded domains and parabolic obstacle problems on finite cylinders, with the additional assumption in the parabolic case that the linearized problem admits a unique solution. In the problem considered here, the cost of time appears as the source term in the equation that the value of waiting satisfies below the bound, so a change in this cost constitutes a perturbation of the source term. Write cε=c0+εγ. Specialized formally to this model, Stojanovic's formula gives the first-order coefficient of the bound as −1/(2c0) times the convolution of the future values of γ with the survival flux at the unperturbed bound. Serfaty and Serra (2018) prove quantitative stability of the free boundary in the elliptic obstacle problem, and De Angelis and Lamberton (2026) obtain a formula of the same implicit-function kind for the boundary's time derivative b′(t). What is new here, to our knowledge, is therefore not the technique itself or the convolution form, but the kernel made explicit, as the density of the time to reach b0 from a start drawn with density proportional to cosh2⁡y on [0,b0] and through its Laplace transform, together with a proof of the expansion for the drift-diffusion model on an infinite horizon, outside the finite cylinders of Stojanovic's theorem. We establish the expansion locally uniformly, using an exact equation that involves no derivative of the bound, with a rate of convergence when the cost perturbation is Hölder continuous.

On smooth-loss sequential inference

Smooth-loss sequential inference. The closest work is Campbell and Engelund (2026), who develop a regularity theory for two-sided stopping problems of a posterior martingale with a nonmonotone time-varying cost, on finite and infinite horizons, including an integral equation. In loss form, their Corollary 4.3 gives the curvature of the value in the continuation region, and their identity (6.14) gives ∂tV on an infinite horizon; translated to our coordinates, these are the two identities of Lemma 2. Their Proposition 8.6 uses the same smooth-fit mechanism to show that under a sinusoidal cost the bound cannot be constant, so it does not converge. Their hypotheses ask for a concave terminal loss, C2 in general and C3 for their time-inhomogeneous regularity results, whose generator image is strictly monotone on each side of a point, and a C1 cost with bounded c′/c. The 0-1 loss of the drift-diffusion model fails these: it has a kink, and its generator image vanishes away from the kink. We therefore prove the identities and the regularity directly. Their own boundary equation is a different, Peskir-type integral equation, and they do not linearize the boundary in a perturbation of the running cost; in their symmetric infinite-horizon setting with smooth losses, however, their two identities and smooth fit would also give a spatially integrated relation like (13). We prove (13) directly for the 0-1 loss and use it to prove the first-order theorem. The explicit kernel and its transform, which we compute from the exit-time law, the weighted look-ahead rule, and the measurements are, to our knowledge, new here. Their Example 8.7 already uses the information-time change of Section 7 for a Gaussian unknown drift.

What the paper leaves open

The paper's own list of limitations, in its order:

  • The leading-order loss formula (Conjecture 11) is a conjecture. Proposition 10 bounds the loss of nearby rules by O(ε2) but doesn't give each rule's loss; Section 6.5 checks the formula numerically.
  • Theorem 7 gives a rate only when the change γ is Hölder or Lipschitz, and its constant is not sharp. In the sweep of Table 2, the fitted swing differs from the first-order prediction by about 0.2% at ε/c0=0.1 and 9.8% at 0.6, but that measures only the swing, not the whole remainder. Discontinuous steps, deadlines, and large changes lie outside the theorem.
  • The drift takes two values. Unknown reliability (through information time) and unequal rewards (through a per-bound envelope argument) are checked numerically, not proved.
  • The paper treats a single decision with a cost of time, not a sequence of trials with a reward rate. With a reward rate, the cost of time is itself set by the policy.

Each is taught where it arises: the conjecture in chapter 7, the sweep in chapter 9 (“Theorem 7 across costs, frequencies, and sizes”), and the two extensions in chapter 11.

Exercises

Worked answers are folded under each question. Nothing is gated.

  1. Show that U=a/b gives δU/U=−δb/b0 to first order.

    Worked answer

    U=a/b, so dU=−adb/b2 and dU/U=−db/b. At first order around b0, δU/U=−δb/b0. Since the bound falls when the cost rises, U rises; and since δb leads the per-moment response, δU leads the cost by the same phase.

  2. What would an urgency signal that implements the per-moment rule predict?

    Worked answer

    No lead: it would track the cost through a/B(c(t)), changing at the moment the cost does. The difference between no lead and a lead of m(c0) time units for slow changes, or a phase that tends to 45∘ for fast ones, is what an experiment could measure.

  3. At high frequency, the phase lead tends to 45∘. Does the lead in time grow too?

    Worked answer

    No. A phase of ϕ radians at angular frequency ω is a time lead of ϕ/ω. As ω grows, ϕ tends to 45∘, but ω grows without bound, so the time lead tends to 0. On the book's computation, at the plates' costs and at frequencies sampled from 0.05 to 3,000, the time lead also falls from each sample to the next, from m(c0) for slow waves; the paper states only the limit. The Palmer numbers show the pattern: 29°, 37°, and 39°, but 162, 103, and 54 ms, at 0.5, 1, and 2 Hz. And “tends to 45∘” is not “at most 45∘”: at c0=1 the phase rises above 45∘ at a finite frequency and falls back below it, as chapter 7 shows.

  4. Convert the printed 37° at 1 Hz to milliseconds. Then do the same for 29° at 0.5 Hz, and explain the result.

    Worked answer

    At 1 Hz, a period is 1000 ms, so 37° is 1000×37/360≈103 ms, the paper's 103. At 0.5 Hz, a period is 2000 ms, so 2000×29/360≈161 ms, but the paper says 162. The paper's value comes from the unrounded phase (a little over 29°); converting the rounded one loses a millisecond. That is why the book quotes the generated numbers and never recomputes from printed, rounded ones.

  5. Why does recomputing the slow-ramp lead from the printed bound 0.95 give 216 ms instead of 217?

    Worked answer

    The paper's figure script starts from the bound 21×0.064×0.71=0.95424, where 0.71 is Palmer and colleagues' fitted bound in their units, which the paper doesn't print. The cost, the look-ahead, and the lead all follow from that bound, and rounding it to 0.95 moves the lead by about a millisecond. The plate in this section does both calculations.

  6. Why does the time unit scale as 1/coherence2?

    Worked answer

    The drift is proportional to coherence (μ=21×coherence per second here), and the noise doesn't change, so σ2/μ2∝1/coherence2: halving the coherence makes the time unit four times as long. A lead computed for the same schedule in the paper's units scales with it. But a cost that changes at a fixed rate in seconds, or a fixed cost per second, becomes a different schedule in the paper's units at another coherence, so its lead has to be recomputed, not just rescaled.

  7. Someone asks whether the weighted look-ahead is a model of how the brain sets its bound. What is the accurate answer?

    Worked answer

    No. It describes what a bound that tracks the optimum would have to compute: to first order, a fixed-weight average over a short stretch of the future. Whether any neural signal does that is the paper's prediction to test (The normative urgency signal leads the cost), and the paper has no data on it.

  8. Specialized to this model, what does Stojanovic's formula give, and is it consistent with Theorem 7?

    Worked answer

    The paper states that it gives the first-order coefficient of the bound as −1/(2c0) times the convolution of γ's future values with the survival flux n. Since n=(∫n)w with ∫n=1/(2c0cosh2⁡b0), that is −12c0⋅12c0cosh2⁡b0(w⋆γ)=B′(c0)(w⋆γ): Theorem 7's coefficient. The paper's own calculation in Section 7.2, in the symmetric case, reproduces it the same way. What the paper claims as new, to its knowledge, is the kernel made explicit and its transform, and the proof for this model on an infinite horizon with a rate.

  9. A referee says: “Isn't this a short step from Campbell and Engelund?” Give the accurate answer.

    Worked answer

    The paper's own answer: their Corollary 4.3 and identity (6.14) are the two identities of Lemma 2, and with smooth fit they would give a spatially integrated relation like the exact equation in their symmetric infinite-horizon setting with smooth losses. But their hypotheses (a concave terminal loss, C2 in general and C3 for their time-inhomogeneous results, whose generator image is strictly monotone on each side of a point, and a C1 cost with bounded c′/c) exclude the 0-1 loss of the drift-diffusion model, which has a kink. Their own boundary equation is a different, Peskir-type integral equation, and they do not linearize the boundary in a perturbation of the running cost. The paper proves the identities and the equation directly for the 0-1 loss, uses them to prove the first-order theorem, and computes the kernel and its transform from the exit-time law.

  10. “Didn't people already know that bounds move before a known change?”

    Worked answer

    Yes, qualitatively. Drugowitsch and colleagues (2012) computed optimal bounds for time-varying costs by backward induction, Barendregt and colleagues (2022) find thresholds that anticipate a known drop in reward, and the best bound in Figure 1, from backward induction, moves before the cost does. What the paper claims, to its knowledge, is an analytic first-order approximation for small changes of general shape, with the kernel explicit.

  11. Why is the reward-rate setting harder?

    Worked answer

    The paper says only that, with a reward rate, the cost of time is itself determined by the policy. In the standard reward-rate setting (background, not in the paper), waiting on this trial delays the next ones, so the cost of time is the reward rate itself, which depends on the policy being chosen. The cost is then an unknown to be solved for together with the bound (a fixed-point problem), not a given schedule.

  12. Which numbers in the paper doesn't its code compute, and what does that mean for this book?

    Worked answer

    The timings in Section 5, which come from the paper's runs on one computer. Everything else, the paper says, its code computes; most of it is written into the generated numbers and tables, and a few values are typed into the text, such as the relative tolerance of 10−5 in Section 7.2. The book reads the generated numbers from those files, quotes typed values as the paper states them, takes a few values from the code and its results files (such as the observer's fitted bound, 0.71, from the figure script, and the unrounded losses behind Table 12), and its check script holds the plates' computations to the generated numbers and the code.

Common misreadings.

  • “The paper shows that the brain implements the optimal bound.”The paper has no data. It says what an urgency signal that implements the optimal bound should do.
  • “The prediction is a theorem.”It is argued formally from Theorem 7, Corollary 8, and Corollary 9, to first order, for small changes on a known schedule. The paper calls it a testable prediction.
  • “The lead is always the mean look-ahead.”Only for slow, nearly linear changes. For a sinusoid, the lead is the transfer function's phase, which tends to 45∘ at high frequency, and in time it is shorter than m(c0).
  • “The phase lead is at most 45∘.”It tends to 45∘ at high frequency. At c0=1 it rises above 45∘ at a finite frequency and falls back below it (chapter 7).
  • “217 ms is a measured lead.”It is a predicted lead, from the paper's reading of the mean observer's fit, for the kind of test the paper proposes: a cost of time that changes within a trial on a known schedule. The paper reports no data on such a lead.
  • “Palmer's observers faced a changing cost of time.”Their data set the scale (the drift and the bound, with the noise fixed at variance 1 per second); the changing cost is hypothetical.
  • “The paper solves Smith and Ratcliff's open problem.”It addresses part of it: what the control would compute, not how it is maintained.
  • “The paper discovered that the first-order correction is a convolution of future costs.”Not new: Stojanovic's (1997) formula, specialized formally, already gives the convolution form. Neither the convolution form nor the perturbation technique is new.
  • “The exact equation is new.”The paper proves it for the 0-1 loss and lists it as a contribution, but its sentences on what is new don't claim it. The paper notes that, in Campbell and Engelund's symmetric infinite-horizon setting with smooth losses, their identities and smooth fit would also give a relation like it.
  • “The weighted look-ahead rule is a fast way to get the optimal bound.”On the paper's code, it is not faster than the dynamic program, and it is not the optimal bound: it is first-order exact near a constant cost (Proposition 13), which is not the same as optimal.
  • “The novelty is certain.”Every sentence in the paper on what is new says “to our knowledge,” and the book keeps the qualifier.
  • “The limitations are minor caveats.”They say which statements are theorems and which are a conjecture, formal arguments, or measurements, and which cases the theorem doesn't cover.
  • “Everything in the paper is regenerated by the code.”The paper says its code computes everything except the timings. Most numbers are written into the generated files, and a few are typed into the paper's text.
