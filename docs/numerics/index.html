The parameters behind each experiment

Table 11 lists, for each experiment, the program that computes its reference, the best policy for a gridded problem in which the choice is forced at a last moment, and that program's grid. Its caption names two kinds of program. The evidence grid is chapter 9's: the dynamic program and the scorer on a grid of evidence values h apart, checking the evidence every Δt (Section 6.1). The belief grid is backward induction over bins of the posterior belief. The horizon is the time of the forced choice.

Two kinds of reference program

Both kinds of program compute the best policy for the same evidence, checked every Δt, and both step back from the forced choice. They differ in what they keep track of.

The evidence grid keeps the evidence x itself, on points h apart. One step back, the value of waiting at x is the average of the next step's value over a Gaussian step with mean +Δt or −Δt, weighted by the belief g(x) that the drift is +1: a mixture of two convolutions. Chapter 9 runs it on your schedule.

The belief grid keeps the belief g, the chance that the drift is +1, at the midpoints of n equal bins on (0,1). Its step from one bin to another comes from the same two Gaussian steps: turn the two beliefs into evidence, x=12log⁡(g/(1−g)), take the mixture of the two evidence densities for that step, weighted by the current belief, and change variables from evidence to belief; each row is then scaled to sum to 1. Appendix C uses 800 bins for the sinusoid test. The program's bound is the first bin above 12 where deciding beats waiting, read between bins.

Equal bins in belief are not equal in evidence. The evidence x has slope 1/(2g(1−g)) in the belief, so a small bin of width 1/n near a belief g covers about 1/(2ng(1−g)) of evidence: about 2/n near 12 and about 50/n near 0.99. Two parts of the program depend on the bins: its step from bin to bin, which samples the step's density at the bins' midpoints, and its bound, read between two bins. The plate solves both kinds of program at a constant cost, with 30 time units left, for six numbers of bins.

At the default cost of 0.02, the belief grid's bound with 25, 50, or 100 bins is more than 0.1 below the evidence grid's. With 800 bins, the two are within 0.01 of each other at every cost on the slider, and with 50 bins, at every cost from 0.1 to 1. The evidence grid's bound is below B(c) at every cost on the slider; chapter 9 measures the shift for checking at intervals.

The horizon

The paper's theorems have no last moment. A program needs one, so each experiment forces the choice at a time given in Table 11's horizon column. Close to the forced choice, waiting has little left to gain, so the bound comes down, to 0 at the forced choice itself.

At a constant cost, the program's step back is the same at every time, so the bound depends only on the time left before the forced choice, and a later forced choice moves the whole curve later by the same time. The plate measures, at each cost, the time left at which the bound is last more than 0.001 from its value with 30 time units left. Guess first, at the default cost of 0.05.

Over the slider's costs, that time left is longer at each lower cost. The paper measures one effect of its own horizon: at a constant cost of 0.02, the constant-cost bound, moved in for checking every Δt, loses 0.0018 points per 100 trials against the best policy with the choice forced at 10, both scored with the choice forced at 10.

What the plate measures, and what it can't show. The time left at which the bound is last more than 0.001, or 0.01, from its value with 30 time units left, at a constant cost, on this page's grid; not the distance from an infinite horizon. For a cost that changes, it also depends on the costs near the end, which the plate doesn't show. The plate scores no losses.

Kernels, two ways

The weighted rule (Algorithm 1, chapter 8) averages the per-moment bounds ahead, B(c(t+s)), with the kernel for its own level at each update. For known reliability, the paper computes that kernel from the eigenfunction series of the survival flux at the flat bound, a sum of exponentials (Lemma 6, chapter 6). For unknown reliability, where the paper carries the kernel over formally (chapter 11), it computes the kernel from a finite-difference generator on 240 interior nodes. The paper's repository tests the series against its Laplace transform.

Precision, seeds, propagation, and tests

Every computation is in double precision. Every random schedule comes from a fixed seed: 2026 for the development schedules, 20261111 for the held-out set, 2027 for unequal rewards, and 7 for the loss check, so a rerun draws the same schedules. The decision-time distributions behind the loss check are not sampled: they come from the same forward propagation of the evidence density that scores every rule (chapter 9). The repository's tests check the constant-cost bounds against the dynamic programs, the scorer against simulated trials, and the kernel series against its Laplace transform.

The rest of Appendix C is grid refinement: eight development schedules rescored on four grids, which move the per-moment losses by at most 0.005 and the weighted ones by at most 0.0017 points per 100 trials from the paper's grid to the finest, and 300 updates of the weighted rule instead of 30. The paper calls the first empirical stability, not an error bound. Chapter 9's last section draws Table 12 and rescores your schedule on three grids; chapter 8 runs the updates on your schedule.

Exercises

Worked answers are folded under each question. Nothing is gated.

  1. Why does Table 11's first row have no evidence step?

    Worked answer

    Its reference is the program over beliefs, whose state is a bin of the posterior belief, not a point of an evidence grid. Its resolution is set by the number of bins, 800, instead.

  2. At a constant cost, why does one solve with the choice forced at 30 give the bound for every earlier forced choice?

    Worked answer

    The program's step back uses the cost only through cΔt, which is the same at every time when the cost is constant. So the value j steps before the forced choice is the same function of the evidence whatever the forced choice's time, and so is the bound. With the choice forced at T, the bound at t is the bound with T−t left, read from the long solve. For a cost that changes, this fails: the steps differ, and each forced choice needs its own solve.

Common misreadings.

  • “The forced choice at 10 is part of the model.”The theorems have no last moment. In the reward-scoring runs on schedules without a deadline, the program ends with a forced choice at 10; the fits use the horizons in Table 11. The fits of Tables 1 and 2 end before their forced choices, while the losses on random schedules are scored up to the forced choice. The paper reports its effect at a constant cost of 0.02 (0.0018 points per 100 trials for the constant-cost bound, moved in for checking every Δt), and in Table 8's check, where at the smallest size of each family most of the weighted rule's predicted loss comes from t>8.
  • “The belief grid and the evidence grid are two models.”They are two programs for the same problem, the evidence checked every Δt: one keeps the belief in bins, the other the evidence on points. On the first plate, with 800 bins and 30 time units left, their bounds are within 0.01 of each other at every cost on the slider.
