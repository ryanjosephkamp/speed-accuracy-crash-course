Your schedule

The paper asks what the best decision rule does when the cost of time changes. A decision maker watches noisy evidence pile up and chooses once the net evidence reaches a bound: a higher bound means slower, more accurate choices. Each moment spent deciding costs c(t), in the same units as the reward for a correct choice, and a schedule says how that cost changes over the trial. Time is measured in units of σ2/μ2, so the evidence drifts at rate +1 or −1, depending on which choice is correct, with noise of variance 1 per unit time.

Pick one of the book's ten schedules, or choose Draw your own and drag across the top chart. Your browser keeps the choice for every page of the book, and each chapter works on it.

Under the schedule is the best bound for it, solved in this page: a dynamic program steps back from a choice forced six time units past the window (or at a deadline), on an evidence grid, the way the paper computes its reference policy (chapter 9). At the ten schedules' starting settings, its bound agrees with the paper's own code to within 10−6, the check's tolerance. Without a deadline, it approximates the paper's bound, which watches the evidence continuously on an infinite horizon; a deadline is a different model, outside the standing assumption, and the program solves it up to the deadline as it stands: checking only every Δt=0.01 moves a constant-cost bound in by about 0.5826Δt, to leading order, and chapter 9 covers the grid and the horizon. When the grid's first step past the center, 0.02, already decides, the bound is below that step: the page places it between the center and the step if the program still waits at the center, and at 0 if it decides at the center too; the paper's code reports 0 in both cases. The dashed curve is the per-moment rule's bound, the shortcut chapter 1 starts from, moved in by the same 0.5826Δt.

Plain words, or the paper's

The dial at the top of every page sets how much of the paper's text you see. Plain shows the book's own words. Exact shows the paper's text for the same result, quoted from its source with line numbers. Both shows the two together. Try it on the rule chapter 1 starts from.

At each moment, the per-moment rule uses the bound that would be best if the current cost of time lasted forever: bpm(t)=B(c(t)), where B(c) is the best bound for a constant cost c. It is exact when the cost is constant, but it doesn't look at what the cost will do next.

The paper's words, in its introduction…

A natural shortcut is to use, at each moment, the constant-cost bound for the current cost:

(1)bpm(t)=B(c(t)),

where B(c) is the optimal bound for a constant cost c. This shortcut, which we call the per-moment rule, is derived by treating the problem as quasi-static. It is exact when the cost is constant, and frozen-cost bounds of this kind are the leading term in large-time asymptotics for related stopping problems with slowly changing costs (Irle et al., 2001; Muravlev, 2020). But it ignores the future.

Guess, then look

Each chapter asks for a guess before it shows an answer, because an answer means more when you've committed to something first. Nothing waits on the guess: Show me always works. Here is the first one, on your schedule. The per-moment rule's bound is drawn; where does the best bound sit at the marked moment? Drag the orange marker, then press Show me.

Whether you guessed above or below the dashed curve depends on what your cost does after the marked moment. A decision still open when the cost changes pays the new cost, so the best bound can start moving before the cost does. One case follows from the paper's results, as an exercise in chapter 5 (the paper doesn't state it): when the cost never falls after a moment, the best bound at that moment sits at or below the per-moment one. Chapter 6 says how far ahead the bound looks.

A proof, a step at a time

Each proof in the book is a strip of steps. Move through them with the slider or the buttons, and the plate beside the proof draws what each step is about; the terms a step changes are lit. This one is a short derivation from the paper: why the best bound for a constant cost c solves sinh⁡(2b)+2b=1/c. Chapter 3 goes through it properly.

Scrub the proof

Step 1 of 3

While the decision maker waits, the value obeys one equation.

Below the bound, LV=c with V′(0)=0, where L=12∂y2+tanh⁡(y)∂y is the generator of Y=|X|.

Step 2 of 3

Multiply by 2cosh2⁡y, and the equation integrates once.

Since (cosh2⁡yf′)′=2cosh2⁡yLf for any smooth f: (cosh2⁡yV′)′=2ccosh2⁡y, so cosh2⁡yV′(y)=c(y+12sinh⁡2y).

Step 3 of 3

At the bound, waiting and deciding meet with the same slope.

Smooth fit, V′(b)=g′(b)=12cosh2⁡b, turns step 2 into c(b+12sinh⁡2b)=12, which is sinh⁡(2b)+2b=1/c.

The paper's derivation

Constant cost. For a constant cost c, the optimal rule stops when Y first reaches a level B(c). In the continuation region, the value solves LV=c with V′(0)=0, so (cosh2⁡yV′)′=2ccosh2⁡y and cosh2⁡yV′(y)=c(y+12sinh⁡2y). Smooth fit at the bound, V′(b)=g′(b)=1/(2cosh2⁡b), then gives

(9)sinh⁡(2b)+2b=1c,

whose root is B(c). The same equation follows from maximizing g(b)−cbtanh⁡b, the expected reward of a flat bound b, since btanh⁡b is the mean decision time.

What the paper proves, and what it doesn't

Not every sentence in the paper is a theorem, and the book keeps the kinds apart. Each entry in the map has a status. The main results are proved for the paper's model under its standing assumption: a cost of time that is continuous and stays between two positive constants, on an infinite horizon, with equal rewards for the two choices. (The paper's Appendix B also derives the constant-cost bounds for unequal rewards, a different model.) Within that, the hypotheses differ: Theorem 7 and Proposition 13 are about small changes around a constant cost, to first order in the size of the change; Lemma 6 and Corollaries 8 and 9 describe the kernel at a constant cost, exactly; and Proposition 10 bounds a reward loss at second order.
