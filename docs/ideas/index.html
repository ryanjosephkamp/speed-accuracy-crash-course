In the paper's units, the evidence is half the log odds that the hidden state is +1, and its size is the one process the proofs work with. This chapter teaches the pieces of probability the later chapters use, on the paper's own objects: the evidence and its time unit, the belief the evidence implies, the folded process and its generator, and the coupling that compares two starting points path by path. A reader who knows stochastic calculus can skim it; a reader who doesn't gets the pieces the proofs use, and no more.

Two of the four claims rest on other work, and the paper says so. That the evidence, seen by the decision maker, is a single diffusion with drift tanh⁡X is a standard result of filtering theory (Peskir and Shiryaev 2006); the book derives the drift and checks consequences of the rest on sampled trials, but doesn't prove it. That the folded equation has exactly one solution for each start and each noise path rests on Skorokhod's lemma (Revuz and Yor 1999), which the book cites; from it, a strip gives the Picard and Gronwall argument the paper states in one sentence, and the paper's own proof that two starts on the same noise stay in order.

Noisy evidence and its time unit

The paper's decision maker must say which of two answers is right. A hidden state H is +1 or −1, each with probability one half, and the evidence Zt drifts at rate μ in the direction of H, with noise of size σ:

dZt=μHdt+σdWt,

where W is a standard Brownian motion independent of H, Z0=0, and μ,σ>0. The decision maker knows μ and σ; only the sign H is unknown.

Two numbers describe the task, and a change of units sets both to 1. Measure time in units of σ2/μ2 and evidence in units of σ2/μ, that is, set t′=μ2t/σ2 and x=μZ/σ2, and the evidence has drift H and noise of size 1. A cost per unit of time converts by the inverse factor: with a=σ2/μ2, the cost per paper time unit at paper time t′ is c′(t′)=ac(at′), so the total cost paid over a decision, ∫c(t)dt=∫c′(t′)dt′, is unchanged. For a constant cost, that is the paper's c′=cσ2/μ2. Nothing is lost: a result in the paper's units converts back by multiplying its times by σ2/μ2.

The time unit has a plain meaning. After time t, the drift has moved the evidence by μt, and the noise has a standard deviation of σt. The two are equal at t=σ2/μ2, so one time unit is how long the evidence takes to reach a signal-to-noise ratio of one.

The plate draws one path of evidence in raw units, for the drift and noise you set, and the same path in the paper's units below it. First, a guess about a real task.

Move either slider and the top chart changes: the drift line, the noise curve, and the path all stretch. The bottom chart doesn't change, because it is the same path measured in the paper's units, where drift and noise always meet at time 1. That is all the change of variables does.

For a sense of scale, the paper takes random-dot motion at 6.4% coherence and the mean observer of Palmer, Huk, and Shadlen (2005), whose drift is 21×0.064 per second with noise variance 1 per second. One time unit is then 1/(21×0.064)2 seconds, 554 ms. The paper notes that the unit scales as 1/coherence2: at half the coherence, each time unit is four times as long. The book's times and costs are all in the paper's units; the last plate of the next section reads your schedule in milliseconds.

From evidence to belief

The evidence is useful because it changes the probability of each answer. In the paper's units, after evidence Xt=x, the probability that H=+1 is

g(x)=11+e−2x.

It depends on where the evidence is, not on how long it took to get there. And it is a logistic function: the log odds of H=+1 are 2x, so the evidence is half the log posterior odds. Evidence and belief determine each other, and the paper works in whichever is easier.

The formula is Bayes' rule, in the strip's first three steps. Before you scrub them, a guess: after one unit of evidence, how sure should the decision maker be?

Scrub the proof

Step 1 of 5

Under either answer, the evidence at time t is normal, and the ratio of the two densities depends only on x.

Start from X0=0, with the noise W independent of H. Given H, Xt=Ht+Wt is normal with mean Ht and variance t, so p+(x)p−(x)=e−(x−t)2/2te−(x+t)2/2t=e2x. For a whole path the ratio is the same, ex−t/2/e−x−t/2 by Girsanov's theorem: it depends on the path only through where it ends.

Step 2 of 5

Bayes' rule with equal priors: the posterior odds are that ratio.

P(H=+1∣evidence)P(H=−1∣evidence)=1/21/2⋅p+(Xt)p−(Xt)=e2Xt.

Step 3 of 5

So the belief is a logistic function of the evidence, and the evidence is half the log odds.

P(H=+1∣Xt=x)=e2x1+e2x=g(x)=11+e−2x, and log⁡g1−g=2x.

Step 4 of 5

The decision maker's best estimate of the drift is tanh⁡X.

E[H∣evidence]=(+1)g+(−1)(1−g)=2g−1=1−e−2X1+e−2X=tanh⁡X. Since H doesn't change, the expected change of the evidence over the next h time units, given what has been seen, is htanh⁡Xt.

Step 5 of 5

What is left is fresh noise, so in the decision maker's view the evidence is one diffusion (a cited result).

The innovation W¯t=Xt−∫0ttanh⁡(Xs)ds is a Brownian motion in the decision maker's own information (Peskir and Shiryaev 2006, Ch. VI), so dXt=tanh⁡(Xt)dt+dW¯t. The plate checks two consequences on 4,000 separate trials, from left sums on steps of 0.01: W¯ has variance 2 at time 2, and its increment from time 1 to 2 has correlation 0 with tanh⁡X1.

Step 4 is where the decision maker's own view comes in. Given H, the evidence drifts at H, which is +1 or −1. The decision maker doesn't know H; in its information, the evidence drifts at its best estimate of H, which is tanh⁡X. The paper writes the evidence in that view as one diffusion,

dXt=tanh⁡(Xt)dt+dW¯t,

where W¯, the innovation, is the evidence minus the drift the decision maker expected. That W¯ is a Brownian motion in the decision maker's own information is a standard result of filtering theory, which the paper takes from Peskir and Shiryaev (2006, Ch. VI) rather than proving; step 5 checks two of its consequences on sampled trials. The stopping problem is posed in this view, because a decision rule can use only the evidence.

(5)g(Xt),g(x)=11+e−2x,

On your schedule. Here is the schedule you chose on the contents page, read in milliseconds if you like, with its best bound read as a belief. The right-hand axis is the left-hand one relabeled by g: a decision made at evidence b is right with probability g(b) (chapter 3 makes this the reward). The program checks the evidence every 0.01 time units, so the evidence can pass the bound between checks, and a decision made on reaching it is right with at least that probability. At a deadline, the choice is forced: every trial still open stops with the evidence x it has, and is right with probability g(|x|). A higher bound means a slower, surer choice, and a bound that falls means the decision maker accepts a lower chance of being right as time goes on.

The milliseconds are for the Palmer observer at the coherence you pick. The bound in evidence and the chance of being right don't depend on that choice; only the clock and the cost per second do. A cost of c per time unit is a cost of cμ2/σ2 per second, in units of the reward for a correct choice, with μ2/σ2 in units of one per second.

Folding the problem in half

The problem is symmetric. Flipping the sign of the evidence swaps the two answers and changes nothing else, and a decision maker who stops chooses the sign of X, which is right with probability g(|X|). So the paper follows only the size of the evidence, Y=|X|. Which way does it drift?

Away from 0, Y drifts away from it at rate tanh⁡Y: the sign of X and the sign of its drift tanh⁡X agree, because tanh is odd. At 0, it can't go lower, and it is pushed back up. Tanaka's formula makes this exact:

dY=tanh⁡(Y)dt+dB+dℓ,

where B is a Brownian motion and ℓ, the local time at 0, grows only while Y=0. So Y is a reflected diffusion on [0,∞). Its generator, the operator whose value Lf(y) at y>0 is the rate at which Eyf(Ys) changes at s=0, is

L=12∂y2+tanh⁡(y)∂y,

with a reflecting boundary at 0. The plate's second chart measures the drift directly: (EyYh−y)/h, the average change of Y over a short time h, divided by h. It is exact, because from the decision maker's view, evidence started at y is y+Ht+Wt with H=+1 with probability g(y). Away from 0, it settles on tanh⁡y as h shrinks. At 0, it grows like 2/(πh), without limit: the push of the local time has no finite rate.

Three facts about g and L are used throughout the paper:

  • Lg=0 for y>0: the belief has no drift while Y>0, because g(X) is a martingale.
  • g(1−g)=1/(4cosh2⁡y), so g′(y)=1/(2cosh2⁡y).
  • (cosh2⁡yf′)′=2cosh2⁡yLf for any twice differentiable f.

Each is a line of calculus. The third turns a second-order equation Lf=q into (cosh2⁡yf′)′=2cosh2⁡yq, which integrates in one step. That integration puts the weight cosh2⁡y into the flat bound (chapter 3), the exact equation (chapter 5), and the kernel's start law (chapter 6); the paper notes that cosh2⁡y is half the speed density of Y, m=2cosh2⁡y (Remark 5). The strip proves the three and then uses the third once.

Scrub the proof

Step 1 of 5

Write the belief through tanh, and the product g(1−g) simplifies.

g(y)=1+tanh⁡y2, so g(1−g)=14(1+tanh⁡y)(1−tanh⁡y)=14(1−tanh2⁡y)=14cosh2⁡y.

Step 2 of 5

Differentiate: the slope of the belief is twice that product.

g′=12sech2y=2g(1−g)=12cosh2⁡y, and g″=−sech2ytanh⁡y.

Step 3 of 5

For y>0, the generator's expression sends g to 0: the belief has no drift while Y>0.

Lg=12g″+tanh⁡yg′=−12sech2ytanh⁡y+12tanh⁡ysech2y=0 for y>0. At y=0, the reflection adds g′(0)dℓ=12dℓ, which is where the value of waiting comes from in chapter 4.

Step 4 of 5

Multiplying by cosh2⁡y turns the generator into a single derivative.

(cosh2⁡yf′)′=2cosh⁡ysinh⁡yf′+cosh2⁡yf″=2cosh2⁡y(12f″+tanh⁡yf′)=2cosh2⁡yLf, for any twice differentiable f.

Step 5 of 5

A first use: the mean time for Y to reach a level b.

m(y)=Eyτb solves Lm=−1 on [0,b) with m′(0)=0 (the reflection) and m(b)=0. Step 4 gives (cosh2⁡ym′)′=−2cosh2⁡y, so cosh2⁡ym′=−(y+12sinh⁡2y), which is m′=−(ytanh⁡y)′, and m(y)=btanh⁡b−ytanh⁡y.

Step 5 is the first of many uses. From 0, the mean time to reach a flat bound b is btanh⁡b; at the flat bound for a cost of 0.1, 1.344, that is 1.17 time units. Chapter 0's strip used the same identity to find the flat bound, chapter 5 uses it to integrate the exact equation, and the formula Eyτ=btanh⁡b−ytanh⁡y returns in the proofs of Theorem 7, Corollary 9, and Proposition 10.

What is proved here, and what is taken from elsewhere. The three facts are direct computations, and the page checks each one. Two starts on the same noise

The proofs in chapters 4 and 5 compare a decision started at y1 with one started at y2, and they do it path by path: run both on the same noise and compare the two paths, not only their averages. That needs the reflected equation to have exactly one solution for each start and each noise path, a strong solution, and it needs the solutions from all the starts to be driven by one noise path B.

The paper takes the first from Skorokhod's lemma (Revuz and Yor 1999, Ch. VI, Lemma 2.1) and gives the argument in one sentence: reflection at 0 is a map on paths that is Lipschitz in the supremum norm, and tanh is Lipschitz, so Picard iteration and Gronwall's lemma give a unique strong solution from every start. The strip spells that sentence out, then adds the paper's one-line reason the paths stay in order. First, a guess.

Scrub the proof

Step 1 of 5

Reflection at 0 is a map on paths: add back the furthest the path has been below 0.

For a continuous path U with U0≥0, Skorokhod's lemma gives the unique pair with Y=U+ℓ≥0 and ℓ nondecreasing from ℓ0=0, growing only when Y=0: ℓt=sups≤t(−Us)+. The map Γ:U↦Y is Lipschitz in the supremum norm, with constant 2.

Step 2 of 5

Picard iteration: reflect, recompute the drift, and reflect again.

Set Yn+1=Γ(y+∫0⋅tanh⁡(Ysn)ds+B). Since tanh is Lipschitz with constant 1, sups≤t|Ysn+1−Ysn|≤2∫0tsupr≤s|Yrn−Yrn−1|ds, so the distances shrink like (2t)n/n! and the iterates converge to a solution.

Step 3 of 5

Gronwall's lemma: the solution is unique, so each start and each noise path give one path.

Two solutions Y,Y~ from the same start on the same B satisfy sups≤t|Ys−Y~s|≤2∫0tsupr≤s|Yr−Y~r|ds, so Y=Y~. The paper runs every start on the same B, so all of them share one filtration.

Step 4 of 5

Two paths that meet are one path from then on, and continuous paths can't pass each other without meeting.

If Ys1=Ys2 at some time s, then after s both solve the same equation from the same point with the same B, and step 3 makes them equal. So for y1≤y2, Yt1≤Yt2 for all t: paths that start ordered stay ordered.

Step 5 of 5

What chapter 4 uses: the lower start collects at least as much local time.

Write Yi=Ui+ℓi. Then U1−U2=y1−y2+∫(tanh⁡Y1−tanh⁡Y2)ds≤0, and since ℓti=sups≤t(−Usi)+, ℓ1≥ℓ2 pathwise.

Order belongs to the coupling, not to the process. With independent noise, the same two starts cross often; switch the plate's noise to see it. What the proofs use is the pair run on one B. In chapter 4, the coupling shows that a start nearer 0 collects at least as much local time, ℓ1≥ℓ2, which is how Lemma 1(a) shows that the value of being allowed to wait, V−g, doesn't increase as the evidence grows. Chapter 5 uses the same coupling to prove smooth fit, Proposition 3(c).

From it, steps 2 and 3 prove existence and uniqueness of the strong solution by the Picard and Gronwall argument the paper states in one sentence, and steps 4 and 5 prove the ordering and the local-time comparison, the paper's own argument. Exercises

Worked answers are folded under each question. Nothing is gated.

  1. Show that x=μZ/σ2, t′=μ2t/σ2 turns dZ=μHdt+σdW into evidence with drift H and unit noise, and find how the cost changes.

    Worked answer

    Multiply by μ/σ2: dx=(μ2/σ2)Hdt+(μ/σ)dWt. Since dt′=(μ2/σ2)dt, the first term is Hdt′. For the noise, define Wt′′=(μ/σ)Wt on the same path; by Brownian scaling, W′ is a standard Brownian motion in the new time, and (μ/σ)dWt=dWt′′. Hence dx=Hdt′+dWt′′. For the cost, write a=σ2/μ2, so t=at′. The total cost doesn't depend on the clock: ∫c(t)dt=∫c(at′)adt′, so the cost per unit of new time is c′(t′)=ac(at′), which is cσ2/μ2 for a constant cost.

  2. Why is σ2/μ2 “the time it takes for the evidence to reach a signal-to-noise ratio of one”?

    Worked answer

    After time t, the drift has moved the evidence by μt, and the noise has standard deviation σt. Their ratio is μt/σ, which equals 1 exactly when t=σ2/μ2.

  3. For the mean observer of Palmer, Huk, and Shadlen (2005) at 6.4% coherence, the drift is 21×0.064 per second with noise variance 1 per second. What is one time unit?

    Worked answer

    σ2/μ2=1/(21×0.064)2 seconds, which the paper prints as 554 ms (The lead in milliseconds for a real task). The paper notes that the unit scales as 1/coherence2; in Palmer and colleagues' proportional-rate diffusion fit, the drift is proportional to coherence, so halving the coherence makes the time unit four times as long.

  4. Derive g(x)=1/(1+e−2x).

    Worked answer

    In these units, the likelihood ratio of a path ending at x under H=+1 against H=−1 is e2x (the Girsanov density for drift +1 against drift −1 is e(+1)x−t/2/e(−1)x−t/2). With equal priors, the posterior odds equal the likelihood ratio, so P(H=+1∣x)=e2x/(1+e2x)=1/(1+e−2x). Taking logs, the log odds are 2x, so x is half the log posterior odds.

  5. Show that 2g(x)−1=tanh⁡x, and explain why that is the drift of X in the decision maker's own view.

    Worked answer

    2g−1=(2−1−e−2x)/(1+e−2x)=(1−e−2x)/(1+e−2x)=tanh⁡x. The decision maker doesn't know H, so its best prediction of the drift H is E[H∣evidence]=(+1)g+(−1)(1−g)=2g−1. The leftover, W¯t=Xt−∫0ttanh⁡(Xs)ds, is a Brownian motion in the decision maker's own information (the innovation process; the paper cites Peskir and Shiryaev, Ch. VI).

  6. Check that Lg=0 and that g(1−g)=1/(4cosh2⁡y).

    Worked answer

    Write g=(1+tanh⁡y)/2. Then g′=12sech2y and g″=−sech2ytanh⁡y, so Lg=12g″+tanh⁡yg′=−12sech2ytanh⁡y+12tanh⁡ysech2y=0. For the product, g(1−g)=14(1+tanh⁡y)(1−tanh⁡y)=14(1−tanh2⁡y)=1/(4cosh2⁡y).

  7. Prove (cosh2⁡yf′)′=2cosh2⁡yLf.

    Worked answer

    (cosh2⁡yf′)′=2cosh⁡ysinh⁡yf′+cosh2⁡yf″=cosh2⁡y(f″+2tanh⁡yf′)=2cosh2⁡y(12f″+tanh⁡yf′)=2cosh2⁡yLf.

  8. Use the identity to find Eyτ for the first time Y reaches a fixed level b.

    Worked answer

    m(y)=Eyτ solves Lm=−1 on [0,b) with m′(0)=0 (reflection) and m(b)=0. The identity gives (cosh2⁡ym′)′=−2cosh2⁡y, so cosh2⁡ym′(y)=−(y+12sinh⁡2y) and m′(y)=−(ysech2y+tanh⁡y)=−(ytanh⁡y)′. With m(b)=0: m(y)=btanh⁡b−ytanh⁡y. This formula returns in the mean decision time (btanh⁡b, from y=0), in the proofs of Theorem 7 and Proposition 10, and in the mean look-ahead (Corollary 9).

  9. Why do two coupled paths that start ordered stay ordered?

    Worked answer

    Suppose Y01≤Y02. If the paths ever met, then from that moment both would solve the same equation, from the same point, with the same B, and pathwise uniqueness makes them equal from then on. Continuous paths can't pass each other without meeting, so Y1≤Y2 for all time.

Common misreadings.

  • “Setting μ=σ=1 is a special case.”It is a choice of units. The change of variables maps the general two-valued-drift problem onto this one exactly, so nothing is lost; results convert back by multiplying times by σ2/μ2.
  • “The drift's size is unknown to the decision maker.”Only its sign H is unknown; its size μ is known. A drift of unknown size is a formal extension in chapter 11 (Unknown evidence reliability, through information time).
  • “The evidence drifts toward the right answer at rate 1, so tanh⁡X is a mistake.”Both are true, in different information. Given H, the drift is H. Without H, which is the decision maker's view and the one the stopping problem uses, the drift is tanh⁡X.
  • “g(X) is the probability of being right.”g(X) is the probability that H=+1. The probability of being right when choosing the sign of X is g(|X|) (Reward, cost of time, and the objective).
  • “Lg=0 means g is constant.”It means g(Y) has no drift while Y>0: g is harmonic for L. At Y=0, the reflection adds g′(0)dℓ=12dℓ, which is where the value of waiting comes from in the proof of Lemma 1.
  • “The coupling is an extra assumption of the paper.”It is the paper's construction on a standard fact. Skorokhod's lemma (Revuz and Yor, Ch. VI, Lemma 2.1) reflects a given path, and with Picard iteration and Gronwall's lemma it gives one solution for each start and noise path; the paper then runs every start on the same noise, and its own argument shows they stay in order. Nothing about the cost enters it.
