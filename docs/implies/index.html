To first order, a cost that rises and falls like a sine wave moves the bound by a smaller swing than the per-moment rule says, and earlier. This chapter derives that from the kernel's closed form in chapter 6, then how far ahead the bound looks on average and why fast changes in the cost are smoothed but not ignored. Its second half turns to reward: why a bound that is a little off loses very little.

Three of the chapter's four claims are proved. The fourth, a formula for how much reward a nearby rule loses, is a conjecture: the paper checks its constant against two exact facts and tests it numerically, and this chapter labels it a conjecture wherever it appears. Of the chapter's results, one number is numerical rather than proved: the largest mean look-ahead over all costs. The readings beside the picture are the paper's measurements on its dynamic program.

A sinusoid in, a sinusoid out

Chapter 6 found the weights the best bound puts on the future and their Laplace transform w~. Apply the theorem to the simplest moving cost, a sine wave γ(t)=Re(Γeiωt) at angular frequency ω, a period of 2π/ω time units. A weighted average of a sine wave is a sine wave at the same frequency, so the first-order bound is one too, and two numbers describe it: how large its swing is next to the per-moment response B′(c0)γ, and how far ahead of that response it runs. Both come from one complex number, the transform at λ=−iω:

β(t)=Re(B′(c0)w~(−iω)Γeiωt).

The bound's swing is |w~(−iω)| times the per-moment swing, and it leads the per-moment response by the phase arg⁡w~(−iω). Before the plate shows them: at ω=8, a period of π/4 time units, how many degrees ahead do you think the bound runs?

At every frequency ω>0, the swing is less than the per-moment swing and the lead lies between 0∘ and 90∘: that is Corollary 8. (At ω=0 the gain is 1 and the lead 0.) At c0=0.1 and ω=8, the closed form gives a swing of 0.358 of the per-moment swing and a lead of 36.7°, and the paper's dynamic program measures 0.358 and 36.4°; chapter 9 covers that experiment, “First-order theory against the dynamic program (sinusoid).” There, the per-moment rule's swing is 2.8 times the best bound's measured swing, and it lags the best bound by 36.4°.

Which way is ahead? B′(c0)<0, so the per-moment response is the cost turned upside down: the bound falls when the cost rises. The first-order bound leads that inverted curve, so it starts to fall before the cost starts to rise.

A lead of φ degrees at frequency ω is a lead in time of φ in radians divided by ω. The plate's readings show that time: it is largest for slow waves, where it approaches the mean look-ahead of the next section, and it falls as the frequency rises, while the lead in degrees can first rise and then fall (at c0=1 it rises above 45∘ and falls back below it, as section 3 shows). That the lead in time falls, and stays below the mean look-ahead, is what the page computes on every cost it offers; the paper doesn't state it.

Why the swing shrinks and the lead is positive: each term of the kernel's series is a decaying exponential, and the transform of each is a simple term in the first quadrant of the complex plane. The proof is four steps; the plate beside each draws the terms as arrows.

Scrub the proof

Step 1 of 4

A wave through the kernel comes out as the same wave, scaled and turned.

For γ(t)=eiωt, (w⋆γ)(t)=∫0∞w(s)eiω(t+s)ds, which is eiωtw~(−iω). Taking real parts with B′(c0) and Γ gives β(t)=Re(B′(c0)w~(−iω)Γeiωt).

Step 2 of 4

Write the transform as a sum of simple terms, one per exponential in the series.

From the series (16), w~(−iω)=∑jaj/(αj−iω) with aj>0, αj=(1+κj2)/2, and ∑jaj/αj=1 (the value at ω=0).

Step 3 of 4

At any frequency above 0, every term points up and to the right.

Each term 1/(αj−iω)=(αj+iω)/(αj2+ω2) has positive real and imaginary parts, so the sum's phase lies strictly between 0∘ and 90∘.

Step 4 of 4

Every term is shorter than it was at zero frequency, so the sum's modulus is less than 1.

Each term has modulus aj/|αj−iω|<aj/αj for ω>0, so |w~(−iω)|<∑jaj/αj=1. (The paper also notes the terms have different phases, which makes the triangle inequality strict.)

How far ahead the bound looks

Corollary 9 takes three limits of the closed form. Two of them are about slow changes and the kernel's mean; the third, for fast changes, is the next section.

(a) w~(0)=1, so a change that is slow next to the kernel is averaged to its present value, which is what the per-moment rule does.

(b) The kernel's mean, the average time ahead that the bound looks, has a closed form:

m(c0)=−w~′(0)=c0[12sinh⁡2b0−b0+2b02tanh⁡b0]=12+2b0(b0tanh⁡b0−1)sinh⁡2b0+2b0.

It tends to 12 as time becomes free (c0→0) and to 0 as time becomes very expensive. Its largest value over all costs, found numerically rather than proved, is about 0.619 time units, at c0≈ 0.035. At c0=0.1, the bound looks 0.546 time units ahead, against a mean decision time of 1.17.

A guess first: when time is nearly free and decisions take longer and longer, how far ahead does the bound look?

Chapter 6's second description of the kernel explains the bottom chart. The look-ahead is the mean decision time from the start law π0(y)=4c0cosh2⁡y, whose weight is concentrated within about a unit of the bound. When time is cheap, a decision from 0 takes b0tanh⁡b0, which grows without bound as c0→0, but almost none of π0's weight is near 0: the distance b0−Y0 is close to exponential with rate 2, and covering it at a drift near 1 takes about half a time unit. The proof is four steps.

Scrub the proof

Step 1 of 4

(a) w~(0)=∫0∞w(s)ds=1 because w is normalized: at zero frequency the weighted average is the value itself, which is the per-moment rule.

Step 2 of 4

Step 4 of 4

Half a unit when time is free, nothing when it is expensive; the peak in between is numerical.

The second form of (22), m=12+2b0(b0tanh⁡b0−1)sinh⁡2b0+2b0, shows m→12 as b0→∞ (c0→0). As c0→∞, b0∼1/(4c0) and m∼23b02→0. The maximum is found numerically.

Part (b) ends with a rule of thumb: for a cost that changes slowly and nearly linearly over the decision's time scale, the best bound at t is, to first order, close to the per-moment bound for the cost at t+m(c0). Then only the kernel's mean matters. Away from that case, its shape matters too: the kernel puts heavy weight on the next instant and has a long, thin tail, which a single shift can't copy. The time lead in the first plate shows one sign of this: on the page's computation (not a result of the paper), it stays below m(c0) at every frequency and falls as the frequency rises, so a shift by m(c0) overshoots any wave that isn't slow.

Fast changes are half-integrated

Part (c) of Corollary 9 is about fast changes. As ω→∞,

|w~(−iω)|∼2c0cosh2⁡b02/ω,arg⁡w~(−iω)→45∘.

The swing falls like ω−1/2, not like ω−1, and the lead tends to 45∘. Both mark a half-integration. Integrating a wave over time divides its swing by ω and turns it by 90∘; the kernel does half of each, because near s=0 it behaves like s−1/2. So fast changes in the cost are smoothed, not ignored.

"Tends to" is the whole claim about the lead. It isn't capped at 45∘: at c0=1, the lead rises above 45∘, falls back below it, and at the highest frequencies the plate scans it is below 45∘ and rising, as it is at the plate's other costs; step 3 of the plate shows by how much. What falls steadily as the frequency rises, on every cost the page computes, is the lead in time; that is a computation, not a result of the paper.

Why the reward barely moves

The chapter's last two claims change the question from where the bound sits to how much reward a misplaced bound loses. Start with a constant cost c and a flat bound b, with a start at 0. It earns

R(b)=g(b)−cbtanh⁡b,

the chance of being right, g(b)=1/(1+e−2b), less the cost of the mean decision time btanh⁡b. The best flat bound B(c) sits at the peak, where the curve is flat, so a small miss costs little: in the exercises you show that R″(B)=−2c, so a miss of δ costs about cδ2.

A guess first: if missing the peak by 0.1 loses some reward, how much does missing it by 0.2 lose?

Proposition 10 makes this hold uniformly over rules when the cost moves a little around a constant. Fix a start y0 below b0 and a constant a. Then, for all small ε, every policy that stops when Y first reaches a continuous bound within a|ε| of b0 earns at most Cε2 less than the best policy for the cost c0+εγ, for one constant C.

The per-moment rule and the first-order bound b0+εβ of chapter 6 both stay within O(ε) of b0, so both lose O(ε2); so does the weighted look-ahead of chapter 8. The proposition bounds the loss; it doesn't say which rule loses less. The proof is four steps.

Scrub the proof

Step 1 of 4

Both decision times are caught between two fixed levels.

Let A=max(a,a0), where a0|ε| bounds the optimal bound's own distance from b0 (Lemma 1(c)). Both the policy's decision time τ^ and the optimal one lie between the hitting times τ± of b0±A|ε|: τ−≤τ^≤τ+.

Step 2 of 4

At the constant cost, the shortfall has two parts.

Itô's formula for V0 gives the policy's shortfall V0(y0)−J0(b^) as E[W0(Yτ^)]+c0E∫0τ^1{Ys≥b0}ds, the waiting value left where it stops and the cost of time spent above b0.

Step 3 of 4

Both parts are of order ε².

Yτ^ is within A|ε| of b0, where W0≤C(b0−y)2 by smooth fit and a bounded second derivative. The expected time spent in a band of width A|ε| next to an absorbing level is O(ε2). The same holds for the optimal policy of cε.

Step 4 of 4

Changing the cost changes both rewards by nearly the same amount.

The perturbation changes each policy's reward by −εE∫0τγ. Between the two policies, these changes differ by at most |ε|M(Eτ+−Eτ−)=O(ε2), since Ey0σz=ztanh⁡z−y0tanh⁡y0 is smooth in the level z.

Here are both rules on your schedule. Each policy is scored by the forward computation chapter 9 describes: the chance of each decision time is pushed forward on the evidence grid, with no sampling. The page writes your schedule as a change around its cost at time 0, c(0)+ε(c(t)−c(0)), and shrinks ε; the proposition then applies with c0=c(0) and γ=c−c(0), when γ is bounded. The plate is a finite version of the proposition's setting: a grid, a forced choice at time 14, and rules moved in for checking every 0.01.

Read the slope-2 line as a reference for the order, not as a ceiling. The proposition says the loss is at most a constant times ε2 for small ε, under its hypotheses; it gives no value for the constant and doesn't say the loss falls at exactly that rate. The first-order bound misses the best bound by O(ε2) for a smooth change, so a loss quadratic in the miss would make it lose order ε4, and on most schedules its loss falls below the display cutoff within a few halvings. The paper measures the weighted rule's order on one smooth shape at 3.7 on one grid and 3.9 on another, a measurement over a short range rather than a proof; chapter 10 covers it, in “How the loss scales with the size of the change.”

The paper's proposition and the paragraphs around it

A bound that misses the optimum loses reward only at second order, because the expected reward is flat at the optimum. Near a constant cost, this holds uniformly over rules.

Proposition 10 (Second-order loss).

Fix a starting point y0∈[0,b0) and a>0. There is a constant C such that, for all small ε, every policy that stops when Y first reaches a continuous bound b^ with ∥b^−b0∥∞≤a|ε| earns at most Cε2 less than the optimal policy for the cost c0+εγ.

The per-moment rule and the weighted look-ahead both qualify, so both lose O(ε2). The proof (Appendix A) combines quadratic contact at the bound, W≤C(b−y)2, with the fact that the time spent in a band of width D next to an absorbing level is O(D2). A sharper statement would say how much each rule loses.

What the proposition covers, and what it doesn't. Small, bounded changes around a positive constant cost, in the paper's continuous-time setting on an infinite horizon; a fixed start below b0; and continuous bounds that stay within a constant times ε of b0. It puts every such rule under the same order; it doesn't rank them. Large steps and deadlines lie outside it, since there a rule's bound can be far from b0. The plate above checks it on a grid with a forced choice at time 14, which the proposition doesn't cover; there, the first-order bound is clipped at 0 for large changes, and a bound of 0 stops every trial still undecided.

How much each rule loses: a conjecture

This section is about a conjecture. The paper states Conjecture 11 without proof. It checks the formula's constant against two exact facts and tests the formula numerically in Section 6.5, which chapter 10 covers.

Conjecture 11 is a sharper statement than Proposition 10, and the paper doesn't prove it. Let f be bounded and Lipschitz. A policy whose bound misses the best bound by η=εf should lose

E[c(τ^)η(τ^)2]

of reward, up to o(ε2), where τ^ is its decision time. In words: the cost of time at the moment of decision, times the squared distance from the best bound then, averaged over when decisions happen.

The constant agrees with two exact facts. At the bound, approached from the continuation side, the waiting value has second derivative (V−g)yy=2c(t), so stopping a distance η short of the bound gives up about c(t)η2. And at a constant cost, the flat bound's reward has curvature exactly −2c at its peak, so a flat miss of δ costs about cδ2, which is what the formula gives when η≡δ.

Two cautions. The conjecture is about a miss of fixed shape εf, but the rules' misses change shape as ε shrinks, so it doesn't by itself say how much less one rule loses than another. And the orders the paper gives for the rules come from a heuristic: a loss quadratic in the miss would make the per-moment rule lose order ε2 and a rule that misses by O(ε2) lose order ε4.

On your schedule, the plate puts the conjecture's prediction for each rule beside the loss the page scores.

In the paper's Table 8, the formula overestimates the per-moment rule's loss by 1 to 5% for smooth schedules and steps up to threefold, and by 36% for 16-fold steps, where the bound's error is no longer small. As the paper says, that table tests the formula for the per-moment rule but says little about the weighted rule: at the smallest change in each family, most of the weighted rule's predicted loss comes from late times, where the best bound collapses toward the forced choice at time 10 and the rules, which ignore it, don't. The plate's late-share reading shows whether the same happens for the first-order bound on your schedule, with the forced choice at 14. Separately, a loss close to the baseline loss of the grid and horizon makes any ratio unreliable, which the plate marks with hollow dots. Chapter 10 tests the formula as the paper does, in “The loss formula, checked” and “How the loss scales with the size of the change.”

Exercises

Worked answers are folded under each question. Nothing is gated.

  1. Why is a positive phase a lead and not a lag?

    Worked answer

    With w~(−iω)=|w~|eiϕ, the first-order response is Re(B′(c0)|w~|Γei(ωt+ϕ)): the per-moment response's shape, scaled by |w~|, evaluated at time t+ϕ/ω. So the bound does now what the per-moment response will do ϕ/ω later. Why ϕ>0: every term of the series, aj/(αj−iω), has a positive imaginary part (step 3). Looking only forward isn't enough by itself: a kernel with all its weight at one delay could shift a sinusoid by any phase.

  2. Read Table 1 at ω=8: what are the closed form's swing and lead, and what does the per-moment rule get wrong there?

    Worked answer

    The closed form gives a swing of 0.358 of the per-moment swing and a lead of 36.7°. Measured on the dynamic program, in the experiment chapter 9 covers, the per-moment rule's swing there is 2.8 times the optimal bound's, and it lags the optimal bound by 36.4°.

  3. The bound leads “the per-moment response.” Does it lead the cost?

    Worked answer

    B′(c0)<0, so the per-moment response is the cost turned upside down: the bound falls when the cost rises. The first-order bound leads that inverted curve. Section 9 turns this around for an urgency signal U=a/b, which rises with the cost: to first order, δU/U=−δb/b0, so U leads the cost itself by the same phase (The normative urgency signal leads the cost).

  4. Derive the first form of m(c0) from m=∫0b04c0cosh2⁡y(b0tanh⁡b0−ytanh⁡y)dy.

    Worked answer

    Write b=b0, c=c0. First part: 4cbtanh⁡b∫0bcosh2=btanh⁡b, since 4c∫0bcosh2=1. Second part: 4c∫0bytanh⁡ycosh2⁡ydy=2c∫0bysinh⁡2ydy=c(bcosh⁡2b−12sinh⁡2b). So m=btanh⁡b−c(bcosh⁡2b−12sinh⁡2b). Replace btanh⁡b by c(sinh⁡2b+2b)btanh⁡b (the constant-cost equation), use sinh⁡2b⋅btanh⁡b=2bsinh2⁡b and 2bsinh2⁡b−bcosh⁡2b=−b: m=c[12sinh⁡2b−b+2b2tanh⁡b], the first form of (22).

  5. Use the second form, m=12+2b0(b0tanh⁡b0−1)/(sinh⁡2b0+2b0), to see why m→12 as c0→0.

    Worked answer

    As c0→0, b0→∞. The numerator 2b0(b0tanh⁡b0−1) grows like 2b02, but the denominator grows like e2b0/2, so the fraction goes to 0 and m→12.

  6. The paper says the bound at t is close to the per-moment bound for the cost at t+m(c0). Why then does shifting the per-moment rule by the mean look-ahead do worse than not shifting on many held-out schedules?

    Worked answer

    The statement is for a cost that changes slowly and nearly linearly over the decision's time scale; there, only the kernel's mean matters, to first order. Away from that case, the kernel's shape matters: it puts heavy weight on the next instant (s−1/2 near 0) and has a long thin tail, which a single shift can't mimic. The paper reports one count over all families without a deadline, with no breakdown by family: on the 180 held-out schedules without a deadline, the shift by the mean was worse than no shift on 83; chapter 10 covers that test.

  7. What does “half-integration” mean here?

    Worked answer

    Integration in time divides a sinusoid's amplitude by ω and delays it by 90∘. A kernel that behaves like s−1/2 near s=0 acts like a fractional integral of order 12: the gain falls like ω−1/2 and the phase tends to 45∘, half of 90∘. (Here it is a lead, because the kernel averages the future.) So fast cost fluctuations are damped, but much less than a full average over a fixed window would damp them.

  8. For a constant cost and a start at 0, show that the reward of a flat bound R(b)=g(b)−cbtanh⁡b has R″(B)=−2c at the optimum, and say what that means for a miss δ.

    Worked answer

    R′(b)=sech2b[12−c(12sinh⁡2b+b)]. At b=B the bracket is 0, so R″(B) is sech2B times the bracket's derivative, −c(cosh⁡2B+1)=−2ccosh2⁡B. Hence R″(B)=−2c, and R(B+δ)=R(B)−cδ2+o(δ2): a miss of δ costs about cδ2.

  9. Does Proposition 10 show that the weighted look-ahead loses less than the per-moment rule?

    Worked answer

    No. It puts both under the same Cε2 ceiling. Which loses less is a measurement (chapter 10) and, for the leading constant, a conjecture (Conjecture 11).

  10. Check the formula of Conjecture 11 for a constant cost and a flat bound that misses by δ.

    Worked answer

    With c constant and η≡δ, the formula gives E[cδ2]=cδ2. The exact loss of a flat bound B+δ is R(B)−R(B+δ)=cδ2+o(δ2) (exercise 8). They agree.

  11. Why can't the conjecture, even if proved, rank the two rules by itself?

    Worked answer

    It is stated for misses of the form εf with f fixed, bounded, and Lipschitz. The per-moment rule's miss is of that form only to first order (εB′(c0)(γ−w⋆γ) plus a smaller remainder, with f Lipschitz when γ is), so applying the formula to it would also need control of how much that remainder changes the reward. The weighted rule's miss is O(ε2) for a smooth change and changes shape with ε; the conjecture gives no error bound that is uniform over such shapes, and an o(ε2) error term alone can't show that a loss is of order ε4. Comparing the two needs more than the conjecture; the paper measures it.

Common misreadings.

  • “The bound rises before the cost rises.”It falls before the cost rises. The lead is relative to the per-moment response, which moves opposite to the cost.
  • “A smaller swing means the bound ignores fast changes.”The swing falls only like ω−1/2 (Corollary 9, part (c)): fast changes are smoothed, not ignored.
  • “The bound looks exactly m(c0) ahead.”m(c0) is the kernel's mean. The bound uses the whole kernel, and the held-out test shows the shape matters.
  • “The maximum 0.619 is proved.”It is found numerically from the closed form, and this page finds it again by the same scan. The limits 12 and 0 are proved.
  • “The look-ahead is about as long as a decision.”Much shorter when time is cheap: at c0=0.1, 0.546 against a mean decision time of 1.17, and the gap grows without bound as c0→0.
  • “Second-order loss means the per-moment rule is optimal to first order.”Its reward is optimal to first order; its bound is not (The per-moment rule's bound error is first order; the corrected rule's is smaller), unless the change γ is constant (Proposition 13). The two statements are about different things.
  • “The weighted rule's loss is proved to be fourth order.”It is a heuristic. Measured, its effective order over a short range was 3.7 on one grid and 3.9 on the other, consistent with four but not a proof (How the loss scales with the size of the change).
  • “The loss formula holds.”It is a conjecture. It overestimates the per-moment rule's losses by 1 to 5% for modest changes, and by 36% for 16-fold steps (The loss formula, checked).
