A decision earns its chance of being right minus the cost of its time; at a constant cost c, the best rule stops when the evidence reaches B(c), where sinh 2b + 2b = 1/c. This chapter sets up the stopping problem the rest of the paper works on: what a decision earns, which costs of time the theory allows, what the chance to wait is worth, and the answer for a constant cost, which every later chapter starts from.

The constant-cost answer is classical, and the paper cites it rather than claiming it. What the chapter works through is the paper's own short derivation, by both of the routes it names, and the slope B′(c) that chapter 6 puts in front of the kernel.

What a decision earns

The decision maker watches the evidence Xt of chapter 2, picks a moment τ to stop, and then chooses the sign of Xτ. Given what it has seen, that choice is right with probability g(|Xτ|), where g(x)=1/(1+e−2x). A correct choice earns 1 and an error earns 0, so g(|Xτ|) is the choice's expected reward. Each moment spent deciding costs c(t), in the same units, on a schedule known in advance. The moment τ may depend on the evidence seen so far but not on evidence still to come: it is a stopping time. The goal is the largest expected reward minus the expected cost of the time spent:

E[g(|Xτ|)−∫0τc(s)ds].

This is one decision, not a run of trials. The cost of time is given in advance; in a reward-rate setting it would depend on the policy itself, and the paper lists that case among its limitations.

The plate runs one path on your schedule and stops it with the simplest rule there is, a flat bound: decide when |X| first reaches b. Drag the bound up and down. A higher bound stops later, so the choice is right more often, and the shaded bill for time grows. The averages under the plate are for checking the evidence continuously, and the page computes them from formulas. Without a deadline, every decision under a flat bound ends at |X|=b, so the average reward is g(b); the average bill is the cost at each moment times the chance that the decision is still open then, ∫0∞c(s)P0(τb>s)ds, with that chance from the series of chapter 6. With a deadline, the decisions still open there are forced to choose at whatever evidence they have, and the bill stops at the deadline.

A flat bound is the best rule when the cost is constant (the flat bound, below). When the cost changes, the best bound generally moves, and the paper gives no formula for it. After Show me, the plate compares the best level held flat that a numerical search finds with the best time-dependent policy, whose bound may change with time, both on the grid of the dynamic program in the value plate below.

Which costs the theory allows

The paper proves its results for costs of time that meet one standing assumption: the cost is continuous, and it stays between two positive constants for all t≥0,

0<cmin≤c(t)≤cmax.

Both levels matter. The upper level keeps the bound away from 0: by Lemma 1(c), the best bound never falls below B(cmax). The lower level does more of the work. It caps the bound at B(cmin), and that cap is what lets the optimal-stopping theorem apply (chapter 4). It makes the value of waiting grow at least like cmin times the squared distance to the bound, Proposition 3(a). And without it, the constant in the bound's Hölder continuity, (cmax−cmin)/cmin, would be infinite (chapter 5).

Check your schedule against it. The plate draws the cost to time 10, past the window the rest of the book draws, because the assumption is about all time. A drawing stops at time 2.5, so for a drawing the plate asks how it goes on.

Three cases are worth keeping apart. The book's steps (Figure 1's rise and fall, the pulse, and the paper's multi-step and piecewise families, whose joins are logistic in log⁡c) are logistic in time with scale 0.05, so they are continuous however steep they look: the assumption holds, and the exact equation of chapter 5 covers them. They lie outside Theorem 7 for a different reason: the theorem is about the limit of small changes, and the paper counts large steps outside it. A deadline makes the cost infinite after it, and the rising cost of Figure 1(c) has no upper level, so the assumption fails for both. The forced choice at time 10 that ends the paper's main experiments (other runs use the horizons in Table 11) is outside the theorems too: it is a last moment, and the theorems are stated with none. In all these cases, the paper says, its rules are heuristics motivated by the theorem, and it judges them by measurement (chapters 9 and 10).

What the assumption does, and what it doesn't. It is a condition the proofs use, not a claim about how costs behave: the structure lemma's band, the regularity of the bound, the exact equation (Proposition 4), and Theorem 7 all assume it. It doesn't put every schedule under Theorem 7, which also needs the change to be small. And the dynamic program still solves a schedule outside it on its grid, with a last moment; what the paper doesn't give there is a proof.

The value of waiting

Fix a start time t and evidence y=|x|. The value V(t,y) is the most a decision maker who starts there can expect to earn, net of the time it will still pay for; the cost s time units after the start is c(t+s):

V(t,y)=supτEy[g(Yτ)−∫0τc(t+s)ds].

Stopping at once is allowed and earns g(y), so V≥g. The difference W=V−g≥0 is what the chance to wait is worth. Where W>0, waiting beats deciding; where W=0, the decision maker stops. The edge between the two regions is the best bound b(t); chapter 4 shows that at each time the stopping set is a single interval, [b(t),∞).

The plate finds V for your schedule by dynamic programming, the way the paper computes its reference policy (chapter 9): it steps back in time from a forced choice, and at each moment and level of evidence it keeps the larger of deciding now and waiting one more step. Before it shades W, guess where waiting is worth the most.

Why there is no last moment. The supremum runs over every stopping time, with no last moment to decide: the decision maker may wait as long as it likes, paying for every moment it waits. That is the infinite horizon, and the paper's theorems are stated on it. Three things come with it. For a constant cost, the problem looks the same from every start time, so V depends on y alone and the best bound is flat, which is what makes the constant-cost answer below a single number. With a last moment, the value would depend on the time left, and the bound would fall toward 0 as that moment neared, as it does before a deadline: set the plate's last moment to time 2.5 to see it. And waiting forever never pays. The cost is at least cmin>0, so a rule whose mean decision time is infinite pays an infinite bill, and the paper only needs rules with Eτ<∞.

A computer can't run to infinity. In the paper's main experiments, the dynamic program forces a choice at time 10 for schedules without a deadline (other runs use the horizons in Table 11), and the paper measures what that does: at a constant cost of 0.02, the constant-cost bound loses 0.0018 points per 100 trials against the optimum for that horizon. The book's solver forces the choice 6 time units past its window. That is an approximation too: the forced choice changes the bound inside the window, more as time gets cheaper and decisions run longer.

The flat bound, two ways

For a constant cost c, the best rule stops when Y=|X| first reaches a fixed level B(c), the root of

sinh⁡(2b)+2b=1c.

The result is classical; the paper cites Wald and Wolfowitz (1948), Shiryaev (1978), and Bogacz and colleagues (2006) for it, and derives it in a few lines, two ways. The first route solves for the value below the bound and asks that it meet the reward for deciding with a matching slope (smooth fit). The second picks the flat bound b that maximizes its own expected reward, g(b)−cbtanh⁡b: every decision under a flat bound ends at |X|=b and is right with probability g(b), and btanh⁡b is its mean decision time from 0 (chapter 2). At c=0.1 the bound is 1.344, from the paper's generated numbers (its text doesn't print it), and the mean decision time is 1.17.

First a guess, on the second route.

Now both routes, a step at a time. Chapter 0 draws the first route's integration on its own; here the plate follows three trial levels through both routes. The routes meet at the last step: the slope of the reward of a flat bound, R′(b), is g′(b) minus the slope at y=b of Vb, the value of following the flat rule at level b. So the reward peaks exactly where smooth fit holds.

Scrub the proof

Step 1 of 5

For a constant cost, the problem looks the same at every start time, so the decision maker stops once Y reaches a flat level b. Below it, the value solves one equation.

LV=c on [0,b), with V′(0)=0 at the reflecting center and V(b)=g(b). For each trial level, the value of following that flat rule, Vb(y)=g(b)−c(btanh⁡b−ytanh⁡y), solves it, since L(ytanh⁡y)=1.

Step 2 of 5

Multiply by 2cosh2⁡y and integrate once from the center: below its own level, every rule has the same slope.

Since (cosh2⁡yf′)′=2cosh2⁡yLf for any smooth f, (cosh2⁡yV′)′=2ccosh2⁡y, so cosh2⁡yVb′(y)=c(y+12sinh⁡2y) on [0,b), for every b.

Step 3 of 5

Smooth fit picks the level: at the bound, the value meets g with the same slope.

V′(b)=g′(b)=12cosh2⁡b makes the left side of step 2 equal 12 at y=b: c(b+12sinh⁡2b)=12, which is sinh⁡(2b)+2b=1/c. Its root is B(c).

Step 4 of 5

The second route: a flat bound b earns g(b) on every decision and pays c for each unit of its mean decision time, btanh⁡b.

From Eyτb=btanh⁡b−ytanh⁡y (chapter 2), the expected reward of a flat bound is R(b)=g(b)−cbtanh⁡b, which is Vb(0).

Step 5 of 5

Set its slope to zero. The same equation comes out, because the reward's slope is the gap between the two slopes at the bound.

R′(b)=g′(b)−c(tanh⁡b+bsech2b)=sech2b[12−c(b+12sinh⁡2b)]. Since c(tanh⁡b+bsech2b)=Vb′(b), R′(b)=g′(b)−Vb′(b): the reward peaks where smooth fit holds.

Scope. This is the answer for a constant cost. When the cost changes, the best bound generally moves, and the paper gives no formula for it. Chapter 5's exact equation reduces to this one at a constant cost, and chapter 6 expands around it for small changes.

How the bound changes with the cost

A higher cost of time means deciding sooner, on less evidence. How much sooner? Guess first.

Differentiating sinh⁡(2B)+2B=1/c in c gives the slope of the constant-cost bound:

B′(c)=−14c2cosh2⁡B(c).

It is negative at every cost, so a higher constant cost always means a lower bound. How much lower depends on the cost. When time is cheap, sinh⁡2b dominates the equation and B(c) grows like 12log⁡(1/c), so as c→0, the drop when the cost doubles tends to 12log⁡2. When time is expensive, B(c)≈1/(4c), so doubling the cost halves the bound.

B′(c0) is not a fitted constant. In Theorem 7, it is the exact factor that turns a small change in the cost, averaged by the kernel, into a change in the bound (chapter 6). The derivation is implicit differentiation, in four steps.

Exercises

Worked answers are folded under each question. Nothing is gated.

  1. Why is the chance of a correct choice g(|Xτ|) and not g(Xτ)?

    Worked answer

    The decision maker chooses the sign of Xτ. If Xτ>0, it says +1, which is right with probability g(Xτ)=g(|Xτ|). If Xτ<0, it says −1, which is right with probability 1−g(Xτ)=g(−Xτ)=g(|Xτ|), using 1−g(x)=g(−x). At Xτ=0, either answer is right with probability 12=g(0).

  2. How does this objective differ from maximizing a reward rate over many trials?

    Worked answer

    Here, the cost of time c(t) is given in advance, and the decision maker optimizes one decision. In a reward-rate setting, the cost of time is itself set by the policy (time spent on this trial is time not spent on the next ones). The paper names this as a limitation (Limitations, as the paper states them) and gives no model for it; the book's gloss is that the cost would then depend on the rule it is meant to choose, a fixed-point problem.

  3. Which of Figure 1's four schedules satisfy the assumption?

    Worked answer

    The 16-fold rise and the fall do: both are logistic in time, so they are continuous and stay between their two levels. The deadline does not: past t=1 the cost is infinite. The ramp in panel (c), 0.1+0.8(t−0.5), does not either, strictly: it is continuous, but it grows without bound, so no cmax exists. In the random-schedule experiments, every schedule without a deadline ends with a forced choice at t=10, and the sinusoid runs end at other finite horizons (The reference policy (dynamic program on the evidence grid), Table 11), so the computed problems are finite; the theorem, though, is stated for an infinite horizon.

  4. What goes wrong if cmin=0?

    Worked answer

    Three things the proofs use. The bound for the lowest cost, B(cmin), is the cap that makes the optimal-stopping theorem apply (Optimal stopping applies after capping the bound); B(0) is infinite. The nondegeneracy estimate W≥cmin(b−y)2 (Proposition 3) becomes empty. And the Hölder constant (cmax−cmin)/cmin becomes infinite.

  5. Why does V depend on the start time t at all?

    Worked answer

    Because the cost changes over time: starting at t means paying c(t+s) for the s-th moment of waiting. For a constant cost, V depends only on y, and the bound is flat.

  6. Show that V≥g, and say what W=V−g measures.

    Worked answer

    The stopping time τ=0 is allowed and earns g(y) with no cost, so the supremum is at least g(y). W is what the option to keep sampling is worth over deciding now; the stopping set is where it is zero.

  7. Derive sinh⁡(2b)+2b=1/c by maximizing R(b)=g(b)−cbtanh⁡b.

    Worked answer

    R′(b)=g′(b)−c(tanh⁡b+bsech2b)=sech2b[12−c(sinh⁡bcosh⁡b+b)]. Setting the bracket to zero gives c(12sinh⁡2b+b)=12, the same equation. The mean decision time btanh⁡b is E0τ from the formula Eyτ=btanh⁡b−ytanh⁡y (The reflected process Y = |X|, its generator, and three facts about g, chapter 2).

  8. How does B(c) behave as time becomes free (c→0) and as it becomes very expensive (c→∞)?

    Worked answer

    As c→0, sinh⁡2b dominates and grows like e2b/2, so B(c) grows like 12log⁡(1/c): slowly. As c→∞, b is small, sinh⁡2b+2b≈4b, and B(c)≈1/(4c), the asymptotic the paper uses in the proof of Corollary 9.

  9. What is the confidence at the bound, and why is it not the bound itself?

    Worked answer

    The bound is a level of evidence X, which is half the log odds. The confidence there is g(B(c))=1/(1+e−2B(c)). The paper states all its bounds on the evidence scale; a text that quotes a probability must convert, and must not call the bound a probability.

  10. Derive B′(c).

    Worked answer

    Differentiate sinh⁡(2B)+2B=1/c in c: (2cosh⁡2B+2)B′=−1/c2. Since cosh⁡2B+1=2cosh2⁡B, the factor is 4cosh2⁡B, so B′=−1/(4c2cosh2⁡B).

  11. Section 7.2 writes the same slope as −∫0∞n(s)ds/(2c0). Check that the two agree.

    Worked answer

    Lemma 6(a) gives ∫0∞n=1/(2c0cosh2⁡b0). Then −∫n/(2c0)=−1/(4c02cosh2⁡b0)=B′(c0). This is the symmetric case of the per-bound slope identity dh/dc=−∫nh/(2c) that the unequal-rewards extension checks numerically (Unequal rewards, one kernel per bound).

Common misreadings.

  • “The decision maker maximizes accuracy.”It maximizes accuracy minus the expected cost of the time spent. Accuracy alone has no best rule: a higher bound always does better, so the cost of time is what makes stopping worthwhile.
  • “The theorem needs a smooth cost.”The exact equation needs only a continuous cost bounded between two positive constants. Theorem 7 is about cε=c0+εγ with γ continuous and bounded, as ε→0: its limit holds uniformly for t in compact sets, and on all of [0,∞) if γ is uniformly continuous. Smoothness enters only through the rate: a Hölder γ with exponent α gives a remainder O(|ε|1+α), and a Lipschitz one O(ε2).
  • “The paper's steps are discontinuous, so its step experiments lie outside the assumption.”The paper's steps are logistic in time (scale 0.05), so they are continuous and bounded, and the exact equation holds for them. They lie outside the theorem because they are large, not because they jump.
  • “V(t,y) is the value of the trial.”It is the value from a start at time t with evidence y. The value of a trial that starts at time 0 with no evidence is V(0,0).
  • “The constant-cost bound is a result of the paper.”It is classical, and the paper cites it. The paper's own work starts from it: the exact equation of chapter 5 reduces to it at a constant cost, and chapter 6 expands around it.
  • “A higher cost of time raises the bound.”It lowers it: B is decreasing, by (10). Expensive time means deciding sooner, on less evidence.
  • “In Theorem 7, B′(c0) is a fitted constant.”It is the exact slope of the classical bound, in closed form.
